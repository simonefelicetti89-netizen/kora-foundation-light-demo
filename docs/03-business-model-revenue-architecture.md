# KORA Business Model & Revenue Architecture

*Status: Approved*

## 1. Business Model Thesis

KORA is not a SaaS company and not a consulting firm. It is an intelligence infrastructure business with multiple monetization layers that activate at different stages of growth.

**Why KORA is not pure SaaS:**
Pure SaaS assumes a self-serve or low-touch sales motion, a standard product that can be activated by any customer without customization, and revenue that scales without proportional headcount growth. In the early phases, KORA is none of these things. The first customers require data mapping, methodology explanation, and human judgment. The first pilots are closer to strategic engagements than software activations.

**Why KORA is not pure consulting:**
Consulting delivers customized, non-repeatable engagements. It does not scale. KORA's ambition is to standardize the intelligence layer so that each successive client is faster, cheaper, and more margin-efficient to onboard. The classification engine, the KORA Index formula, and the reporting templates are assets that compound. Consulting does not compound. KORA must.

**What KORA actually is:**
A platform-enabled intelligence business. The first commercial motion is assessment-led (high touch, high price, clear deliverable). The recurring motion is software-led (subscription, dashboard, automated reporting). The premium motion is certification-led (methodology validation, external review, credentialed output). The ecosystem motion activates later when the network is large enough to generate proprietary value.

## 2. Revenue Streams

---

**Stream 1 — Foundation Light Setup / Assessment Fee**
What it is: A one-time fee for the initial KORA analysis engagement.
Who pays: The company.
Why they pay: They need a credible diagnosis of their people program before redesigning it, reporting it to the board, or presenting it to investors.
When it starts: Phase 1. This is the first revenue KORA generates.
Horizon: Short-term entry point.
Risks: Must be positioned as the opening of an ongoing intelligence relationship, not "just a report."

---

**Stream 2 — Foundation Annual Subscription**
What it is: Annual recurring revenue for access to the Foundation platform — recurring ingestion, KORA Evolution tracking, quarterly reports, workforce intelligence.
Who pays: The company.
Why they pay: They want to track improvement, produce quarterly evidence, and demonstrate that their program is working.
When it starts: Phase 2.
Horizon: Medium to long-term. The recurring engine of KORA's core business.
Risks: Churn if the company does not see improvement in their KORA Index. Prevention: clear milestone moments in the subscription cycle.

---

**Stream 3 — Governance Annual Subscription**
What it is: Premium subscription for financial governance, policy rules, audit trail, and decision intelligence layer.
Who pays: The company. Finance and Legal become active stakeholders at this tier.
Why they pay: Budget accountability, board reporting readiness, multi-provider management, ESG regulatory compliance.
When it starts: Phase 3.
Horizon: Long-term. Highest-margin recurring product in the core tier stack.
Risks: Long sales cycles at large enterprises. Legal requirements vary by jurisdiction.

---

**Stream 4 — Certified Annual Certification Fee**
What it is: Annual fee for the KORA Certified assessment, external validation, badge and evidence package.
Who pays: The company.
Why they pay: External communication credibility — investors, ESG agencies, talent markets, regulatory compliance.
When it starts: Phase 4. Cannot be sold until the methodology is validated and the advisor network exists.
Horizon: Long-term, high-value, defensible recurring revenue. Certification is sticky.
Risks: Cannot be sold on an unproven methodology. Premature claims damage credibility.

---

**Stream 5 — External Advisor Review Fee**
What it is: Per-engagement fee for a KORA-authorized advisor to conduct the Certified assessment review or strategic methodology audit.
Who pays: The company, or shared between company and advisor.
Why they pay: Independent validation required for external credibility.
When it starts: Phase 4.
Horizon: Medium to long-term. Scales with the advisor network.
Risks: Advisor quality control is critical — one bad review damages the entire Certified tier.

---

**Stream 6 — Partner Certification Fee**
What it is: Fee paid by welfare providers, training companies, ESG program operators to receive KORA Certification for their services.
Who pays: Partners (providers).
Why they pay: Access to the KORA company network; commercial and positioning advantage.
When it starts: Phase 4–5, when KORA has enough active companies that network membership has commercial value.
Horizon: Long-term. Significant when network effect is established.
Risks: Premature certification without live company network produces no value for partners.

---

**Stream 7 — Advisor Academy / Training / Licensing**
What it is: Training and accreditation program for external consultants who want to become KORA-authorized advisors.
Who pays: Individual advisors or their employing firms.
Why they pay: Access to KORA methodology, brand and client network. Differentiation in the HR/ESG advisory market.
When it starts: Phase 4–5.
Horizon: Long-term. Can become meaningful as the advisor ecosystem grows.
Risks: Academy content requires investment. Quality must be a genuine gate, not just revenue.

---

**Stream 8 — Enterprise Custom Integration Fee**
What it is: One-time or annual fee for custom integrations between KORA and a large enterprise's existing systems (HRIS, ERP, LMS, welfare platform APIs).
Who pays: Enterprise clients (typically >5,000 employees).
Why they pay: They cannot use flat file uploads at enterprise scale.
When it starts: Phase 3–4.
Horizon: Medium-term. Not scalable on its own — integration fees fund connector cost.
Risks: Custom integrations create technical debt. Should only be built when serving multiple clients.

---

**Stream 9 — API Access / Methodology Licensing**
What it is: Access to KORA's classification API or methodology for companies wanting to integrate KORA scoring into their own platforms.
Who pays: HR tech companies, consulting firms, enterprise IT teams.
Why they pay: KORA's taxonomy and scoring methodology adds an impact intelligence layer without building from scratch.
When it starts: Phase 5.
Horizon: Long-term, high-margin if standardized. Requires IP protection.
Risks: Licensing too early undermines credibility. Licensing to competitors can commoditize the core product.

---

**Stream 10 — Benchmark and Anonymized Data Intelligence**
What it is: Industry or sector-level intelligence derived from anonymized, aggregated KORA data across multiple companies.
Who pays: Investors, research organizations, policymakers, HR associations.
Why they pay: No one else has structured, verified, longitudinal human impact data at company level.
When it starts: Phase 5. Requires minimum 2–3 years of longitudinal data from sufficient client base.
Horizon: Long-term. A strategic asset and defensible competitive advantage.
Risks: Privacy — even anonymized data requires rigorous de-identification and explicit consent frameworks.

---

**Stream 11 — Premium Reporting Packages**
What it is: Add-on reporting — enhanced PDFs, board-presentation decks, investor communication packages, ESG appendix documents.
Who pays: The company, as an add-on to subscription.
Why they pay: Standard KORA reports are functional; premium packages are narrative, branded, formatted for specific audiences.
When it starts: Phase 2–3.
Horizon: Short to medium-term. Margin-efficient once templates are built.
Risks: Must be clearly differentiated from standard reports.

---

**Stream 12 — Territory or Ecosystem Programs**
What it is: Contracts with local governments, chambers of commerce, or industry associations to produce territorial human impact intelligence using aggregated KORA data.
Who pays: Public bodies, industry associations, foundations.
Why they pay: Evidence of human impact of companies in their territory.
When it starts: Phase 5. Requires critical mass of client companies in a territory.
Horizon: Long-term. Strategically important for public positioning.
Risks: Public sector sales cycles are slow. Should be pursued only when company-side network is large enough.

---

**Stream 13 — Optional Managed Program Fee**
What it is: Fee for KORA actively managing a company's people programs — allocating budgets, coordinating with providers, running initiatives.
Who pays: The company.
When it starts: Phase 5 or beyond. Requires the full ecosystem layer.
Horizon: Long-term. High revenue potential but high operational complexity.
Risks: Approaches financial intermediation. Requires careful legal structuring. See Section 3.

---

**Stream 14 — Transaction-Related Revenue (Conditional)**
What it is: Percentage fee on transactions flowing through KORA — welfare budget distributions, partner payments, marketplace purchases.
When it starts: Only in Phase 5, and only if legally and commercially structured to support it.
Horizon: Long-term. Potentially large but requires significant infrastructure, regulatory compliance and trust.
Risks: Highest-risk revenue model. Requires payment service provider licensing, clear separation of company funds from KORA operating revenue, and careful legal structuring. Do not pursue until full infrastructure is in place.

## 3. Separation of Revenue vs Managed Funds

*Conceptual only. Not legal advice.*

**The risk:**
As KORA evolves, company welfare budgets may flow through its platform to pay partners or distribute welfare credits. If KORA holds these funds, even briefly, it may be classified as a payment institution or financial intermediary by regulators (particularly under EU frameworks like PSD2).

**The distinctions KORA must maintain:**

*KORA fee revenue:*
What companies pay KORA for intelligence, software, reporting, governance and certification services. Clean, unambiguous B2B revenue. KORA earns this by delivering value and has no obligation to pass it on.

*Company service funds:*
Budget companies allocate to fund their people programs. This money belongs to the company and its workers. KORA should not hold it, route it, or earn a margin on it unless the legal structure explicitly permits this.

*Partner payouts:*
Payments from company budgets to welfare providers, training companies or other partners. If these flow through KORA, it must be structured as a payment facilitator, not as KORA operating revenue.

*Advisor fees:*
Fees paid to KORA-authorized advisors. KORA may charge a platform commission on advisor engagements, distinct from its core service fee.

*Certification revenue:*
Fees paid to KORA for certification assessment, badge and evidence package. Clearly KORA service revenue, not fund management.

**The operating principle:**
KORA should earn money by delivering intelligence, governance, reporting and certification. It should not earn money by routing company funds to providers unless it has deliberately built the legal and financial infrastructure to do so. In early phases, KORA should sit alongside budget management, not inside it.

## 5. Pilot Commercial Offer

**Offer name:** KORA Impact Diagnosis

**What it is:**
A structured, 60-day engagement in which KORA analyzes a company's existing HR, welfare, training and ESG data and delivers the company's first KORA Impact Report — a structured picture of human impact across the five pillars, with a prototype KORA Index, pillar balance, activation analysis, and financial governance summary.

**Scope:**
- One company, one analysis period (previous 12 months or most recent complete year)
- Existing data only — no new infrastructure required
- Data sources accepted: HR headcount export, welfare program usage records, training completions, ESG/CSR initiative declarations, people initiative records, budget allocations (optional)
- No integration work — flat file upload only
- Delivered in Italian and/or English

**Duration:** 60 days from data receipt to final report delivery

**Deliverables:**
1. Data quality assessment
2. Action classification report (how existing data maps to the five pillars)
3. Prototype KORA Index with pillar breakdown
4. Pillar balance visualization
5. Activation rate analysis
6. Financial governance summary
7. Data confidence score
8. Executive summary presentation (10–15 slides, board-ready)
9. Activated KORA Foundation Light dashboard (12 months access)

**Price range:** €10,000–€18,000

**Success criteria:**
- The company receives a prototype KORA Index it can explain to its board
- The company sees its pillar balance and understands which dimensions are strong or weak
- The company can identify at least two concrete strategic implications
- The company understands the cost-to-impact ratio of its current spending

**Conversion path:**
At conclusion, the company is offered Foundation annual subscription and/or Governance add-on if applicable. The engagement is positioned from the beginning as "the first year of a multi-year relationship, not a one-time project."

## 6. Unit Economics Logic

*Directional assumptions only. All require validation with real pilots.*

**Onboarding effort per Foundation Light client (early phase):**
- Data intake and quality review: 8–16 hours
- Action classification and pillar mapping: 12–20 hours (decreases as classification engine matures)
- KORA Index calculation and validation: 4–8 hours
- Report production: 8–12 hours
- Total: approximately 32–56 hours per client

At Italian professional service rates (€80–€150/hour senior analysts), delivery cost is approximately €2,500–€8,500 per client.

**Gross margin potential:**
At €10,000–€18,000 per Foundation Light engagement with delivery costs of €3,000–€8,000, gross margin per client is approximately 40–70%.

**The margin improvement lever:**
As the classification engine and report templates become standardized, human delivery effort per client drops dramatically. Target: reduce Foundation Light delivery from 40+ hours to under 15 hours within 12–18 months through automated classification, standardized templates, and documented playbooks.

**Recurring margin potential:**
Foundation annual subscriptions, once the platform is built, carry gross margins of 70–85% at scale — standard for B2B SaaS with human support.

**What must be standardized to scale:**
1. Data ingestion formats — standard company templates, not arbitrary formats
2. Action classification taxonomy — documented, version-controlled, automatable
3. Report templates — executive report, board pack, ESG evidence package
4. Onboarding playbook — each step from contract to final report documented

## 7. Strategic Risk

**Too much consulting, not scalable:** If every client requires fully customized analysis, margin never improves. Prevention: standardize aggressively from the first client.

**Too cheap, loses premium positioning:** At €2,000–€5,000, Foundation Light signals commodity. Upselling to €30,000/year Foundation becomes very difficult. Prevention: price to communicate category value, not just deliverable value.

**Too complex, slow sales cycle:** If the first commercial conversation requires explaining the full KORA ecosystem, sales cycles exceed 6 months. Prevention: first commercial offer explainable in one sentence and one page.

**Too dependent on certification too early:** A failed certification promise destroys trust in the entire platform. Prevention: do not sell Certified until methodology is mature and at least one full internal assessment cycle is complete.

**Too dependent on transaction fees:** Requires financial regulation territory before legal infrastructure exists. Prevention: keep transaction revenue out of scope until Phase 5 with explicit legal structuring.

**Confusing client funds with revenue:** If company welfare funds sit in KORA accounts without proper legal structure, regulators may classify KORA as a payment institution. Prevention: design the platform to sit alongside budget management, not inside it.

**Selling ESG certification before methodology is validated:** A sophisticated ESG analyst will ask hard questions about the formula and verification process. If answers are not ready, the sale creates reputational damage.

## 8. Recommended Revenue Roadmap

**Phase 1 — First paid assessments (Months 1–9)**
Revenue: Foundation Light setup fees. Target: 3–8 paying pilot clients in Italy.
Goal: validate classification engine, report format, KORA Index formula and commercial positioning.
Revenue range: €30,000–€150,000 in setup fees.
Milestone: first client that signs a Foundation subscription.

**Phase 2 — Recurring Foundation subscriptions (Months 6–18)**
Revenue: Foundation annual subscriptions (converted from Phase 1 pilots).
Target: 5–15 active Foundation subscribers.
Revenue range: €90,000–€500,000 ARR.
Milestone: first client that renews without being asked.

**Phase 3 — Governance upsell (Months 12–24)**
Revenue: Governance annual subscriptions.
Target: 3–8 Governance clients from Foundation base.
Revenue range: €100,000–€600,000 incremental ARR.
Milestone: first Governance client that uses audit trail to defend a budget decision to their board.

**Phase 4 — Certified + Advisor layer (Months 18–36)**
Revenue: Certification fees, advisor review fees, advisor academy.
Target: first 3–5 Certified companies; first cohort of 10–20 authorized advisors.
Milestone: first company that uses KORA Certified badge in external investor communication.

**Phase 5 — Partner / Ecosystem / Data Intelligence (Month 30+)**
Revenue: Partner certification fees, API/methodology licensing, benchmark intelligence, territory programs.
Goal: activate network-effect layer and begin generating proprietary data intelligence revenue.

## 9. Founder Decision Points

- **First price:** One number, not a range. The range is for internal calibration.
- **Pilot duration:** 60 days or 90 days? Choose before the first proposal.
- **Dashboard or report only:** Does Foundation Light include interactive dashboard access, or only a report?
- **Advisor review included or not:** Is external advisor review part of Foundation Light?
- **Mention Certified now or later:** Discuss the vision, but do not promise delivery dates before methodology is ready.
- **Financial governance: basic or central:** Headline promise or secondary feature? Determines which buyer persona is primary.
- **Pricing logic:** Employee-count, fixed engagement, or company-size bands? Choose one before the first proposal.

## 10. Recommendation

**KORA's first monetization strategy: sell strategic intelligence, not software.**

The first commercial offer should be a strategic engagement — a people-impact assessment delivering a concrete executive artifact (KORA Impact Report) and an activated platform (Foundation Light dashboard). Price: €10,000–€18,000 as a fixed engagement fee converting naturally to a Foundation annual subscription.

The full commercial architecture must be visible in every conversation from day one. Prospects buying Foundation Light must understand they are entering a platform, not a one-time tool.

The single most important thing KORA must do in Phase 1 is produce a KORA Index that a company's CHRO trusts enough to show to their board. That trust is the commercial asset that every subsequent revenue stream depends on. Everything else follows from that.
