## 1. Validation Objective

The purpose of the 90-day post-MVP validation phase is not to close deals. The purpose is to generate structured, investor-grade evidence that KORA's value proposition is real, that a definable customer profile exists, and that a credible commercial pathway to Foundation Light is present.

At the end of 90 days, KORA must be able to make the following claims — with evidence, not narrative:

### 1.1 Companies Understand the Problem

The market validation phase begins by proving that the problem KORA solves is recognized as real by target organizations. This means stakeholders in target companies — HR directors, CFOs, ESG leads, CEOs — articulate the problem in their own language without the founder needing to describe it for them. When a CHRO says "we have no idea what our welfare budget is actually producing" unprompted, that is a problem-recognition signal.

A validated problem recognition does not require KORA to exist. It requires the pain to be named independently.

### 1.2 Stakeholders Recognize KORA as a Credible Solution

The second claim is that when KORA is presented, stakeholders with real authority and relevant mandates recognize it as a credible response to the problem they described. This is not the same as liking the idea. Credibility means the stakeholder would not be embarrassed to bring KORA to their CFO or board as a serious proposal.

Credibility signals include: asking detailed implementation questions, requesting a demo for their team, asking about data requirements, asking about methodology validation, referencing a specific ESG obligation KORA could help satisfy.

### 1.3 At Least Some Companies Are Willing to Test Foundation Light

The third claim is that KORA can demonstrate at least two to five companies that have advanced beyond expressed interest and are actively considering — or have agreed to — a Foundation Light pilot. These companies have identified internal stakeholders, discussed what data they would provide, and confirmed the feasibility of a pilot engagement.

The threshold for this claim: at least Stage 6 (Budget Discussion) or Stage 7 (Commitment Signal) on the doc 14 pipeline.

### 1.4 Willingness to Pay Emerges

The fourth claim is that at least some stakeholders have signaled — even non-bindingly — that they would allocate budget to KORA. This does not require a signed contract. It requires that budget has been mentioned by the stakeholder in a context that is meaningful, with a Confidence Level of Intent of at least Medium in the doc 14 framework.

Willingness-to-Pay Score ≥ 46 on at least three records constitutes a minimum evidence threshold. A Willingness-to-Pay Score ≥ 66 on at least one record constitutes a strong signal for investor purposes.

### 1.5 ICP Begins to Crystallize

The fifth claim is that patterns in the data allow the founder to describe a specific company profile — sector, size, maturity, trigger — that is meaningfully more receptive than average. The ICP is not defined at the start of validation. It emerges from it. By day 90, the ICP Fit Distribution (doc 14, Section 4.13) should reveal which company profiles consistently score higher on perceived value, engage further in the pipeline, and signal greater willingness to pay.

An ICP that cannot be named is a validation failure, even if individual conversations feel positive.

### 1.6 Objections Become Measurable

The sixth claim is that the most common barriers to adoption have been identified, named, and structured. This requires Objection Tags in doc 14 to be populated across enough records that patterns emerge. By day 90, the Objection Frequency Map should identify the top three to five objections reliably. A founder who knows the top objections — and has structured responses to them — has market intelligence. A founder who is still surprised by objections does not.

### 1.7 Investor-Grade Validation Evidence Is Collected

The final claim is that the validation dataset is structured, versioned, and auditable — meeting the evidentiary standard that sophisticated investors and advisors would consider credible. This requires the doc 14 intelligence layer to be populated consistently throughout the 90 days: not just pipeline stage counts, but PVS distributions, Trust Scores, objection clusters, ICP Fit analysis, and Founder Conviction Delta trend.

An investor who asks "how do you know the market wants this?" must be answered with structured data, not anecdote.

## 2. What Counts as Real Validation

The most dangerous failure mode in early-stage validation is mistaking polite engagement for genuine market demand. KORA's validation methodology must distinguish rigorously between signal strength levels. All signal assessments are captured in the doc 14 intelligence layer.

### 2.1 Signal Strength Framework

#### Weak Signal

A weak signal indicates awareness or polite interest. It does not indicate problem recognition, value alignment, or any intent to act. Weak signals must be logged but must not be counted toward validation evidence.

**Examples of weak signals:**
- "This is interesting" at the end of a 15-minute call
- A LinkedIn connection request after an event pitch
- A stakeholder forwarding the KORA one-pager to someone without follow-up
- A positive reaction to the KORA concept without any questions about implementation
- Agreement that "companies should measure impact" in the abstract
- A friend or advisor saying they like the idea
- A stakeholder mentioning KORA at a conference without arranging a meeting

**Doc 14 indicators of a weak signal:**
- Engagement Stage: 1–2 (Contacted / Responded)
- Perceived Value Score: 0–25
- Trust Score: 0–40
- Founder Conviction Delta: 0 or negative

#### Moderate Signal

A moderate signal indicates genuine interest and some alignment between KORA's value proposition and the stakeholder's perceived needs. It suggests the conversation is worth continuing. It does not constitute validation.

**Examples of moderate signals:**
- A second meeting is requested by the stakeholder (not just agreed to when proposed)
- Stakeholder describes their own problem in terms that map to KORA's solution
- Stakeholder introduces KORA to a colleague in the same meeting
- Stakeholder asks specific questions about methodology, classification, or data requirements
- Stakeholder references a specific internal context: "we've been trying to solve this for two years"
- Stakeholder requests a demo for their team

**Doc 14 indicators of a moderate signal:**
- Engagement Stage: 3–4 (Engaged / Interested)
- Perceived Value Score: 46–65
- Trust Score: 41–60
- Founder Conviction Delta: +1 to +2

#### Strong Signal

A strong signal indicates that the stakeholder has moved from interest to exploration. They are testing whether KORA is feasible for their organization. This involves internal action — involving colleagues, raising the topic with their team, discussing data or operational requirements. Strong signals are validation-grade evidence when accompanied by a reliable Trust Score.

**Examples of strong signals:**
- Data sharing has been discussed — the stakeholder has asked what data KORA needs and whether they have it
- The budget owner has been involved in a conversation, directly or indirectly
- Stakeholder has introduced KORA internally and arranged a follow-up with a broader team
- Stakeholder has provided their own HR or welfare data informally to understand fit
- Pilot logistics have been discussed: who would be involved, what the timeline might be, what success would look like
- Stakeholder requests a proposal or pricing information
- Stakeholder mentions KORA to their board or CFO

**Doc 14 indicators of a strong signal:**
- Engagement Stage: 5–6 (Pilot Consideration / Budget Discussion)
- Perceived Value Score: 66–80
- Willingness-to-Pay Score: 46–65
- Internal Champion Strength: 61–80
- Trust Score: 61–80
- Founder Conviction Delta: +3 to +4

#### Hard Validation

Hard validation is the highest evidentiary standard. It means a company has made a concrete, real-world commitment — of time, data, resources, or money — to KORA. Hard validation counts toward investor-grade PMF evidence. Each instance of hard validation is a milestone.

**Examples of hard validation:**
- A signed or verbally committed pilot agreement with timeline and scope defined
- A Letter of Intent (LOI) from an authorized signatory
- A paid pilot, even at a nominal amount — money changes the nature of the commitment
- Actual data provided by the company for KORA to ingest and analyze
- Budget formally allocated by the company's finance function for a KORA engagement
- A company willing to serve as a named reference or case study

**Doc 14 indicators of hard validation:**
- Engagement Stage: 7–8 (Commitment Signal / Active Validator)
- Perceived Value Score: 81–100
- Willingness-to-Pay Score: 66–100
- Internal Champion Strength: 61–100
- Trust Score: 61–100
- Budget Range Indication: populated with at least Small tier
- Confidence Level of Intent: High or Verified
- Founder Conviction Delta: +4 to +5

### 2.2 The False Validation Trap

Free pilots, when granted to avoid the price conversation, are not hard validation — they are expensive relationship maintenance. A company that accepts a free pilot but has never discussed budget, does not have a committed internal champion, and has not provided real data is at Stage 5 at best, not Stage 8. The free pilot must be resisted unless it is structured as a strict, time-limited diagnostic with a contractual path to a paid engagement defined in advance.

## 3. Target ICP Hypotheses

The Ideal Customer Profile is a hypothesis at the start of validation. It must be tested, refined, and eventually confirmed or replaced by the data that emerges from the market. The following hypotheses define the starting point for KORA's first ICP and the alternative profiles to test in parallel.

### 3.1 Primary ICP Hypothesis — Foundation Light v0.1

**Label:** Italian Mid-Market Company with Active People Investment

**Profile:**

| Dimension | Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Geography | Italy (primary), with potential extension to German-speaking Europe and Benelux |
| Company size | 100–1,000 employees — large enough to have meaningful welfare/HR/ESG programs, small enough that the founder can reach the decision-maker directly |
| Sector | Manufacturing & Industry, Professional Services, Financial Services & Insurance, Technology & Digital — sectors with established welfare budgets, ESG reporting pressure, or CSRD obligations |
| Welfare / HR maturity | Company already spends on welfare, training, or people programs — minimum €50,000 annually in budget — and has some form of internal HR or People function |
| ESG / CSRD pressure | Company is subject to CSRD reporting obligations or voluntary ESG commitments, or has received board or investor pressure to demonstrate social impact |
| Data availability | Company has data that KORA can ingest: HR records, welfare provider reports, training logs, ESG declarations — in structured or semi-structured form. Not necessarily clean or formatted — but present. |
| Decision-maker accessibility | The CEO, CHRO, or CFO is reachable within two degrees of the founder's existing network, or through a warm introduction from a trusted advisor |
| Innovation openness | The company has demonstrated openness to new approaches — through past technology adoption, participation in industry innovation programs, or leadership language that references transformation and data |
| Urgency trigger | A current or near-term trigger exists: ESG audit, board reporting cycle, welfare budget review, leadership change, regulatory deadline, or investor diligence process |

**Why this ICP is the primary hypothesis:**
Italian mid-market companies represent the most accessible first market for Foundation Light. The welfare system (Flexible Benefits / Welfare Aziendale) creates an existing budget allocation that KORA can analyze. CSRD adoption among Italian companies of this size is creating genuine urgency around social impact evidence. The founder network is concentrated in the Italian market, enabling warm introductions. And the 100–1,000 employee range is large enough to have meaningful data without requiring enterprise procurement cycles.

### 3.2 Secondary ICP Hypotheses to Test in Parallel

**Secondary A: Large Italian Enterprise (1,000–5,000 employees)**
Higher contract potential. Longer sales cycle. More stakeholders to navigate. Test in parallel to calibrate pipeline speed and decision-maker access patterns at scale. Indicator: ICP Fit Score components for company size and decision-maker accessibility will be lower; ESG pressure and data availability components will be higher.

**Secondary B: Welfare / HR Consultant or Advisory Firm**
Not a direct KORA end customer, but a potential distribution channel and credibility multiplier. A large HR consulting firm that recommends KORA to ten client companies is worth more than one direct sale. Track separately in the pipeline with high Strategic Relationship Value flags.

**Secondary C: International Mid-Market (German-speaking Europe, Benelux)**
Lower regulatory familiarity with Italian welfare structures; different ESG context. Test to understand whether KORA's value proposition translates across jurisdictions before Foundation is built. Early signals in this segment inform internationalization sequencing.

### 3.3 ICP Disqualifying Signals

The following signals, when observed, indicate a company is likely outside the primary ICP and should be deprioritized unless there is an exceptionally high Strategic Relationship Value reason to continue:

- No structured welfare or training budget (people investment is informal or negligible)
- Fewer than 50 employees (data will be too thin for meaningful KORA analysis)
- No HR function or named HR/People lead (no internal owner for KORA's output)
- No ESG or social impact mandate from board or leadership (no urgency driver)
- Procurement process requires minimum 12 months from first contact to contract (Foundation Light cycle is incompatible)
- Company explicitly refuses data sharing even at an exploratory level (Foundation Light requires data)
- ICP Fit Score below 40 after a full discovery conversation

## 4. Market Timing and Structural Tailwinds

### 4.1 Why Timing Is a Strategic Variable

Market timing is not a fixed fact — it is a hypothesis that must be tested. KORA's thesis is not merely that human impact intelligence is valuable in the abstract. It is that the conditions for market adoption are present now in a way that they were not three years ago and may not be three years from now. The validation phase must actively test this hypothesis by watching for structural tailwind signals in every stakeholder conversation.

A product can be correct in concept and wrong in timing. Launching an analytics layer when companies do not yet have data, launching a measurement framework when regulation has not yet demanded measurement, launching an intelligence product when internal buyers lack the mandate or budget to act — all of these produce real conversations and zero commercial outcomes. The 90-day validation phase must verify not just that the problem exists, but that it is becoming urgent now.

### 4.2 Structural Tailwinds Supporting KORA

The following structural forces are creating conditions favorable to KORA's entry into the market. Each is observable in stakeholder conversations and must be actively tracked.

---

**CSRD and the Social Reporting Imperative**

The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) entered EU mandatory application beginning with fiscal year 2024 for large listed companies, expanding progressively through 2026–2028 to cover mid-market companies above 250 employees. CSRD requires companies to report not just environmental data but detailed social data — workforce composition, compensation equity, training hours, health and safety incidents, working conditions, community impact.

The critical gap CSRD creates for KORA: it mandates social disclosure but does not specify how to make social data meaningful, comparable, or defensible. Companies can comply superficially with CSRD by listing activities. But auditors, investors, and sophisticated regulators are already asking for evidence of outcomes, not just activities. KORA's impact intelligence layer is positioned precisely in this gap — between the mandate to report and the absence of methodology for reporting credibly.

**Observable signal:** A stakeholder who mentions CSRD obligations or external audit pressure in the first meeting without being prompted is surfacing a genuine urgency trigger. Log as high-urgency ICP signal.

---

**Growing Scrutiny of Welfare and HR Spending Effectiveness**

Italian and European companies collectively spend billions annually on welfare programs, flexible benefits, training initiatives, and people programs. CFOs are increasingly applying ROI logic to this spend. "We spend X on welfare — what do we get?" is a question that has become significantly more common in board discussions over the last two years.

The welfare industry has historically been structured around benefit availability, not benefit impact. A company can demonstrate that it has a large welfare catalog and a high enrollment rate — but neither tells the CFO whether the spend is producing measurable organizational value. KORA operates precisely in this gap.

**Observable signal:** A CFO or HR leader who uses the language of ROI, efficiency, or cost-per-outcome in relation to people programs is expressing a structural need for what KORA produces. Log as high WTP signal.

---

**Increasing Demand for Defensible ESG Evidence**

ESG ratings, investor ESG questionnaires, and B2B client ESG due diligence are proliferating. Companies that previously issued social impact narratives without evidence are now being asked to demonstrate methodology and auditability. "Show me the data behind your S score" is the new standard. A company that cannot produce traceable, methodology-versioned evidence for its social impact claims faces reputational and commercial risk.

This is directly KORA's value proposition for ESG leads: not just impact measurement, but impact measurement that is auditable, versioned, and defensible under scrutiny.

**Observable signal:** An ESG lead who mentions audit pressure, LP questionnaires, or investor ESG diligence is surfacing a real commercial need. Log as high-urgency ICP and high SRV signal.

---

**Shift from Activity Metrics to Outcome Metrics**

The HR and ESG analytics market has been dominated by activity metrics: participation rates, training hours completed, benefit redemption rates, headcount diversity ratios. These are easy to produce and easy to present — but they measure what happened, not what it produced. Sophisticated HR leaders, board members, and investors are beginning to draw a sharp distinction between the two.

This represents an irreversible maturation of the market's analytical expectations. KORA benefits from being on the outcome side of this shift — but the shift is still early, which means some buyers are ready for it and others are not. Identifying which stakeholders have already crossed this threshold is a core function of the discovery conversation.

**Observable signal:** A stakeholder who explicitly distinguishes between "participation rates" and "actual impact" — or who expresses frustration with existing reporting because it only measures activity — is indicating outcome-metric readiness. Log as high PVS potential and high ICP fit signal.

---

**Rise of People Analytics Maturity**

The last decade produced a significant expansion of people analytics capabilities inside mid-to-large companies. HR functions that once tracked headcount and absence now routinely work with engagement scores, attrition predictors, and workforce planning models. This creates a growing population of HR leaders who are analytically literate and actively seeking more sophisticated data on their workforce — including data on the impact of their people programs.

These HR leaders are KORA's highest-readiness audience. They already speak the language of data-driven HR. They understand that correlation between welfare investment and productivity is worth measuring. They have the internal credibility to push for new analytics tools.

**Observable signal:** A CHRO or HR leader who references existing people analytics tools (Workday, SAP SuccessFactors, Visier) and describes what's missing from those tools is a high-readiness indicator. Log as high ICP fit.

---

**Fragmentation of Current Welfare/HR/ESG Ecosystems**

The current landscape of welfare management platforms, HR information systems, ESG reporting tools, and training platforms is highly fragmented. A typical mid-market Italian company manages welfare through a third-party welfare platform, HR data through an HRIS, training through an LMS or external providers, and ESG reporting through spreadsheets or dedicated consultants. None of these systems talk to each other. No system produces cross-domain impact intelligence.

This fragmentation is not a problem that will be solved by any of the existing platform vendors — it is structural, because each vendor is optimized for its own domain. KORA does not need to replace any of these systems. It needs to sit above them as the intelligence layer that translates their collective outputs into unified impact intelligence.

**Observable signal:** A stakeholder who describes "too many systems, no unified picture" — or who references the effort involved in preparing an integrated view for board reporting — is articulating the exact problem KORA solves. Log as high PVS indicator.

---

**Lack of a Unifying Impact Intelligence Layer**

Despite the proliferation of HR, welfare, and ESG tools, no product currently offers what KORA is defining: a methodology-based, privacy-preserving, cross-domain human impact intelligence layer that translates heterogeneous organizational data into a structured, comparable, and auditable intelligence framework. This is not a gap that competing products partially fill — it is a category that does not yet exist at the product level.

This is simultaneously KORA's strongest asset and its primary risk. Defining a new category requires substantial market education. Early adopters must be self-aware of the gap they are experiencing, not merely receptive to KORA's description of it. The validation phase must test whether enough companies are already articulating the need without being prompted.

**Observable signal:** When a stakeholder describes a problem that maps to KORA's value proposition before KORA has been explained — this is the highest possible market timing signal. Log as exceptional Founder Conviction Delta.

---

**Increasing Investor Focus on Human Capital**

Institutional investors — particularly ESG-oriented funds, impact investors, and the LP base of leading European VC firms — are dramatically increasing their scrutiny of human capital as a value driver. The SEC's human capital disclosure rules, the SASB Human Capital standards, and the growing body of research linking workforce investment to stock performance are creating an investor demand for credible human capital intelligence that current HR reporting cannot satisfy.

For KORA, this creates a dual opportunity: companies want to demonstrate human capital quality to investors, and investors want standardized, comparable human capital data across portfolio companies. Foundation Light addresses the first. The ecosystem tier (corporate intelligence and portfolio analytics) would address the second.

**Observable signal:** A CEO or CFO who references investor pressure to demonstrate human capital quality — or an investor contact who asks whether KORA produces comparable data across companies — is surfacing a structural alignment between KORA's roadmap and investor demand.

---

**AI and Data Infrastructure Making Cross-System Intelligence Feasible**

The current generation of AI infrastructure — embedding models, semantic data mapping, structured output generation, and LLM-based classification — makes it practical to ingest heterogeneous data from multiple source systems, map it to a unified ontology, and produce structured intelligence outputs at a cost and speed that was not feasible five years ago. This is the technical tailwind that makes Foundation Light's ingestion model viable without enterprise-scale engineering investment.

**Observable signal:** This is primarily an internal signal, not a stakeholder signal. It supports the feasibility argument in investor and advisor conversations.

---

**Organizational Pressure to Demonstrate ROI on People Investment**

In the post-2022 environment of cost discipline, talent market normalization, and growing pressure on HR to demonstrate strategic value, CHROs and CPOs face unprecedented internal scrutiny of their budgets. The question is no longer whether to invest in people — it is whether the investment is producing measurable organizational value. This creates a genuine internal pressure that KORA addresses directly.

**Observable signal:** A CHRO or CPO who references internal pressure from the CFO or CEO to justify people program spending is expressing a real urgency driver. Log as high urgency ICP signal and strong WTP indicator.

---

### 4.3 KORA Is Attempting to Define a New Analytical Category

KORA is not entering an existing market. There is no "human impact intelligence" software category that buyers are already searching for. KORA is attempting to define that category from scratch — which has different implications from competing within an established space.

**What this means for the validation phase:**

Category creation requires demonstrating that the problem is real before demonstrating that the solution is good. A buyer who does not yet feel the problem will not buy the solution, regardless of how well KORA solves it. The 90-day validation phase is therefore primarily a problem-recognition exercise, not a solution-selling exercise. Conversations that end with stakeholders articulating the problem in their own words — without KORA having named it for them — are the highest-value outcomes of the discovery phase.

Category creation also means that the competitive analysis is not "KORA vs. X." It is "KORA vs. the current absence of a coherent solution." The existing alternatives are spreadsheets, welfare platform dashboards, ESG consultants, and general HR analytics tools. None of these are competitors in the traditional sense — they are the incumbent inadequacy that KORA replaces.

**The naming challenge:** If stakeholders cannot quickly locate KORA in a mental category they already have, they must be able to create a new mental category and place KORA in it. The validation phase must test which framing produces that category creation most reliably. "Impact intelligence" is KORA's category definition. Whether the market is ready to receive it — or whether a simpler entry framing ("the intelligence layer for your welfare and ESG programs") works better in practice — is an empirical question the validation phase must answer.

### 4.4 Why Timing Matters for Investors

For investors evaluating KORA, market timing is not a soft consideration — it is a structural bet. Investors want to know: is the market pulling KORA forward, or is KORA pushing against a resistant market?

A company that is too early must educate the market for years before revenue appears. A company that is correctly timed finds the market already halfway to its door. The structural tailwinds listed in Section 4.2 represent the investor-facing case that KORA is entering at the right moment: CSRD is creating mandatory demand for what KORA produces; the analytics infrastructure now makes KORA technically feasible at startup economics; the HR analytics market has matured enough to produce a literate buyer base; and the fragmentation of existing ecosystems means no incumbent is positioned to fill this gap.

### 4.5 The Risk of Being Too Early

Being too early is as dangerous as being too late — and harder to detect, because early markets produce enthusiastic conversations and no revenue. The clearest sign of being too early is universal interest and zero urgency. If every stakeholder says "this is great, we'll need this eventually," KORA is too early for this ICP at this moment.

The antidote to too-early risk is urgency testing: every discovery conversation must surface whether there is a specific, near-term trigger that makes KORA relevant now. If no triggers can be found across twenty conversations, the timing hypothesis needs revision — not the product.

### 4.6 Observable Market Timing Indicators

The following indicators should be monitored throughout the validation phase. Their frequency and intensity in stakeholder conversations is the market's direct signal about whether KORA's timing is correct.

| Indicator | What it measures | Target observation frequency |
|---|---|---|
| CSRD references in first conversation | Regulatory urgency is already present and named | ≥ 40% of conversations with companies ≥ 250 employees |
| HR leaders asking ROI questions unprompted | Outcome-metric shift is happening inside the market | ≥ 30% of CHRO/HR Director conversations |
| Investor references to human capital analytics | Investor demand is pulling companies toward measurement | ≥ 50% of investor-type conversations |
| ESG teams requesting evidence defensibility | Audit pressure is creating demand for KORA's auditability layer | ≥ 35% of ESG lead conversations |
| Welfare budget scrutiny mentioned by CFO/Finance | Cost pressure is creating ROI demand for welfare spend | ≥ 25% of CFO conversations |

If the observed frequency of these indicators falls significantly below target across twenty or more conversations, the market timing hypothesis should be formally re-evaluated — not as a failure, but as a structured learning signal that informs when and how KORA accelerates its go-to-market.

## 5. Stakeholder Map

Different stakeholders inside the same company have different concerns, different mandates, and different signals to offer. Contacting the wrong stakeholder first — or using the wrong angle — wastes both time and relationship capital. The following map defines the engagement logic for each contact type in the doc 14 vocabulary.

---

### 5.1 CEO / Founder / Owner

**What they care about:** Strategic differentiation, board and investor narrative, ESG reputation, organizational culture, talent attraction. At mid-market companies, often personally involved in HR and welfare decisions.

**KORA angle to use:** KORA makes the human value of the company visible and credible — for investors, for clients, for talent. The KORA Index is a strategic instrument, not a reporting tool. Position as: "You've invested heavily in your people. For the first time, you can prove what that produced."

**Signal to capture:** Do they personally feel the problem? Do they reference investor pressure, board questions, or competitive dynamics? Does the conversation shift from polite listening to active problem-sharing?

**Objection to expect:** "We already have ESG reporting." (KORA is not ESG reporting — it is what goes inside it, with evidence.) "This sounds complex." (Foundation Light is a diagnostic, not a system.) "We don't have time for new projects." (This is a timing objection — log and schedule follow-up for a better moment.)

**Validation value:** CEO/Owner involvement is a strong ICP and credibility signal. A CEO who self-refers the problem is one of the most reliable indicators of genuine market need.

---

### 5.2 CHRO / HR Director / Chief People Officer

**What they care about:** Demonstrating the ROI of people programs, justifying welfare and training budgets to the CFO and board, workforce analytics, talent retention, KORA's ability to produce HR reporting evidence. The CHRO is typically KORA's primary internal champion.

**KORA angle to use:** "You've built programs that matter. Now you can show what they produced — in a language the board understands." Frame KORA as the evidence layer behind the HR function's existing work. KORA does not replace the CHRO's judgment — it makes that judgment visible and defensible.

**Signal to capture:** Does the CHRO describe frustration with current reporting? Do they reference specific welfare or training initiatives they cannot measure? Do they ask about data formats and ingestion — a sign they're mentally testing feasibility?

**Objection to expect:** "We already track participation metrics." (Participation is activity, not impact — KORA measures what the activity produced.) "Our data is messy." (Foundation Light is designed for messy data — confidence scoring handles quality variance.) "I'd need to involve finance." (This is a champion-to-budget-owner signal — a positive progression indicator, not a barrier.)

**Validation value:** CHRO-initiated discussions are the most natural path to pilot conversion. A CHRO with Internal Champion Strength ≥ 61 is the most reliable predictor of a Foundation Light pilot.

---

### 5.3 CFO / Finance Director

**What they care about:** Cost control, fiscal optimization, ROI evidence for welfare spend, CSRD/ESG cost of non-compliance, risk reduction, audit readiness, investor relations.

**KORA angle to use:** Lead with the financial governance angle — KORA's budget intelligence layer shows cost per outcome, not just cost per activity. For CFOs under CSRD pressure: "KORA is the evidence layer that makes your social impact declarations defensible in a CSRD audit." For welfare-heavy companies: "You're spending significant budget on welfare. KORA tells you what's working and what isn't — with the evidence to defend the allocation."

**Signal to capture:** Does the CFO reference budget reviews or welfare cost scrutiny? Do they ask about pricing and contract terms — a Willingness-to-Pay signal? Do they ask about audit trail and methodology versioning — a credibility and compliance signal?

**Objection to expect:** "We already manage welfare spend." (Managing spend and understanding impact are different — KORA bridges the gap.) "What's the ROI of KORA itself?" (This is the core commercial question — have a clear, specific answer: cost per outcome intelligence, CSRD readiness, reduced audit exposure.)

**Validation value:** CFO involvement without CHRO initiation is unusual but strategically significant. A CFO who self-initiates a KORA conversation is a strong signal of urgency and budget authority alignment.

---

### 5.4 ESG / Sustainability Director

**What they care about:** CSRD compliance, social impact evidence, ESG rating improvement, stakeholder reporting credibility, avoiding greenwashing exposure, supply chain social standards.

**KORA angle to use:** KORA provides the Social pillar evidence layer that ESG reporting frameworks require but rarely specify. It answers the question regulators and investors are increasingly asking: not just "what programs did you run" but "what impact did those programs produce, and how do you know?" Position as: "KORA makes your S credible."

**Signal to capture:** Are they already under a CSRD obligation or preparing for one? Do they reference external auditors requesting impact evidence? Do they ask about KORA's methodology versioning and auditability — a strong credibility signal?

**Objection to expect:** "Our ESG consultant handles this." (KORA is the intelligence layer — the consultant produces the report. They are not competitors.) "We're not under CSRD yet." (Upcoming obligation — frame as preparation.) "What certifications does KORA have?" (Methodology validation is a Phase 4 feature — the honest answer is that Foundation Light provides the diagnostic baseline that will later be certifiable.)

**Validation value:** ESG leads are KORA's fastest path to board-level visibility and CSRD urgency. They are often not the final budget decision-maker but are frequently the strongest internal champions for a KORA pilot.

---

### 5.5 Innovation Manager / Chief Innovation Officer

**What they care about:** New approaches, pilot programs, technology adoption, being first-mover in their sector, demonstrating strategic leadership.

**KORA angle to use:** Position Foundation Light as a first-mover intelligence opportunity — "You'd be among the first companies in your sector to have structured human impact intelligence." Innovation managers respond to novelty, methodological rigour, and the opportunity to lead rather than follow.

**Signal to capture:** Do they propose a fast-track pilot timeline? Do they name an internal colleague they want to involve immediately? Do they reference a budget they control for innovation programs?

**Objection to expect:** "This is interesting but not core to our innovation roadmap." (Innovation manager interest without CHRO or CFO involvement is high-SRV but low-commercial-urgency — track as strategic asset, deprioritize for pilot conversion.) "How does this fit with our existing HR tech stack?" (Integration question — Foundation Light does not require integration, only data export.)

**Validation value:** Innovation managers are excellent for pilot experimentation and case study development but are rarely the final commercial decision-maker. Treat as champion-building target — they mobilize internally but require CHRO or CFO involvement for pilot commitment.

---

### 5.6 Welfare Manager / Benefits Manager

**What they care about:** Welfare program effectiveness, employee satisfaction with benefits, fiscal optimization of welfare budget, compliance with welfare fund rules.

**KORA angle to use:** "Your welfare programs generate data every day. KORA transforms that data into intelligence about what your employees are actually doing — and what it's producing." Position Foundation Light as the analytical layer above the welfare platform, not a replacement for it.

**Signal to capture:** Do they describe frustration with welfare platform reporting? Do they reference the volume of data they have but cannot analyze? Do they ask about specific welfare providers or fund structures KORA can work with?

**Objection to expect:** "Our welfare platform already has analytics." (Platform analytics measure consumption, not impact — they show what was used, not what it produced.) "I don't have budget authority for this." (Welfare Manager is a champion, not a decision-maker — ask who the budget owner is.)

**Validation value:** Welfare Managers are frequently the most informed about data availability and program structure. They are the ideal technical co-pilot in a pilot engagement. Their involvement signals practical feasibility, not commercial commitment.

---

### 5.7 Legal / Compliance

**What they care about:** Data protection, regulatory compliance, audit risk, GDPR alignment, CSRD obligations, methodology defensibility, contractual clarity.

**KORA angle to use:** KORA's privacy architecture is a core product principle — pseudonymization by design, employer-aggregated outputs only, no individual surveillance. Position the methodology versioning as a compliance asset: "Every KORA output carries the version of the methodology that produced it — that is an audit trail, not a black box."

**Signal to capture:** Do they raise GDPR concerns proactively? This is a positive signal — they are taking KORA seriously enough to evaluate it legally. Do they ask about the pseudonymization architecture? Do they request the methodology documentation?

**Objection to expect:** "We need to see your data processing agreement before we proceed." (This is a legitimate stage-appropriate request — have a DPA template ready.) "How is individual data protected?" (This is the core KORA privacy proposition — answer confidently and in detail.) "We can't share employee data with a third party." (Clarify: KORA requires only aggregate-level or pseudonymized data exports, not individual personal records.)

**Validation value:** Legal involvement at early stages is a strong organizational commitment signal — it means the company is taking the KORA engagement seriously enough to involve compliance. It is not a barrier; it is a progression indicator.

---

### 5.8 Advisor / Consultant

**What they care about:** Staying ahead of the market, offering clients innovative solutions, credibility with their client base, building a differentiated advisory offering around impact intelligence.

**KORA angle to use:** Position KORA as a methodology and intelligence layer that enhances the advisor's own offering. "Your clients are asking about impact measurement. KORA gives you a structured, credible answer — and gives your clients the first real evidence they've had." Advisors who adopt KORA as a recommended tool become distribution channels.

**Signal to capture:** Do they name specific clients who would benefit? Do they ask about partnership or referral arrangements? Do they volunteer to introduce KORA to their client network?

**Objection to expect:** "I already recommend other tools." (Understand which tools — identify the gap KORA fills.) "My clients won't pay for another platform." (KORA is not a platform clients operate — it is a diagnostic service. The entry cost is low relative to the evidence it produces.)

**Validation value:** Advisors have high Strategic Relationship Value regardless of their direct commercial potential. A single advisor who introduces KORA to three clients can unlock three pipeline entries simultaneously. Track advisor records with SRV weighting and invest in these relationships even when direct conversion timelines are uncertain.

---

### 5.9 Investor / Accelerator

**What they care about:** Portfolio company value creation, ESG reporting for their own LPs, deal flow, sector positioning, evidence-based investment thesis support.

**KORA angle to use:** Two distinct angles depending on the investor type. For investors with portfolio companies: "KORA gives you structured, comparable human impact intelligence across your portfolio — the social data your LP reports are missing." For investors evaluating KORA as an investment: the Foundation Light validation data itself is the pitch — stage progression, ICP crystallization, WTP signals, Active Validators.

**Signal to capture:** Do they ask about the methodology in analytical terms — a signal of intellectual seriousness? Do they name specific portfolio companies that would benefit? Do they ask about KORA's own traction and validation evidence?

**Objection to expect:** "The market isn't ready." (Probe this specifically — which market signals led to that assessment? It may reveal a specific objection to address, or it may be a Founder Conviction Delta –3 event.) "We already have ESG tools in our portfolio." (Understand which tools — KORA's differentiation is impact intelligence, not ESG reporting.) "Too early." (Acknowledge timing; establish a structured follow-up cadence at a defined milestone.)

**Validation value:** Investor-type stakeholders have dual value: they can become commercial customers (for portfolio intelligence) and investment partners. Track their pipeline stage separately from commercial stakeholders. Investor Interest Ratio (doc 14, Section 4.11) tracks this parallel pipeline.

## 6. Outreach Strategy

The outreach strategy for the 90-day validation phase is defined by channel quality, not channel volume. A warm introduction from a trusted advisor is worth twenty cold LinkedIn messages. The strategy must concentrate on the highest-quality access points first, then expand as the pipeline develops.

### 6.1 Channel Priority Hierarchy

**Priority 1 — Warm Introductions from Trusted Network**

The highest-conversion channel. A mutual contact who can make a credible introduction to a CHRO, CFO, or CEO eliminates the biggest barrier in B2B validation: credibility. Warm introductions produce meetings with higher ICP Fit, higher Trust Score from the first interaction, and faster stage progression.

*Approach:* Map the founder's full existing network. Identify everyone who has direct relationships with target ICP companies. Contact each individually with a specific ask: "I'd like to connect with [role] at [company type] — do you know anyone I should speak with?" Do not send generic "I'm building something interesting" messages. Be specific about who, why, and what the 30-minute conversation would accomplish.

*Priority:* Highest. Every warm introduction is the first action in every day of the validation phase.

---

**Priority 2 — Advisor and Mentor Introductions**

KORA advisors, board members, and domain mentors (HR, ESG, fiscal/welfare, impact measurement) have existing credibility in the sectors KORA targets. An introduction from an ESG advisor to a sustainability director carries institutional weight that a cold outreach cannot match.

*Approach:* Brief advisors specifically on the validation objective and the ICP profile. Give them a clear, short request: "If you know any HR Directors or ESG leads at mid-market Italian companies who are wrestling with impact measurement, I'd value an introduction." Do not rely on advisors to proactively generate introductions — make specific, targeted asks with context.

*Priority:* High. Advisor introductions are the second daily priority after warm network mapping.

---

**Priority 3 — HR and ESG Consulting Networks**

HR consultants, welfare consultants, and ESG advisors who work with mid-market companies are both potential champions and referral channels. They already have established trust relationships with KORA's ICP. A consultant who understands KORA's value can introduce it to multiple client companies simultaneously.

*Approach:* Identify the top five to ten HR/welfare/ESG consulting firms in the Italian market. Request introductory conversations explicitly framed as "understanding what your clients are asking about in impact measurement." Do not pitch KORA in the first meeting — understand their perspective first, and allow KORA to emerge as a relevant solution. The Founder Conviction Delta from these conversations will be high-information regardless of commercial outcome.

*Priority:* High. These conversations produce both direct pipeline entries and market intelligence.

---

**Priority 4 — University and Academic Networks**

Economics, management, HR, and sustainability faculties at Italian universities have networks that touch both corporate HR functions and ESG leadership. Professors who advise companies on compensation, welfare, or ESG frequently have direct relationships with CHROs and sustainability directors.

*Approach:* Identify researchers and faculty whose work intersects with human capital measurement, welfare economics, ESG reporting, or organizational psychology. Request a meeting framed as methodology discussion — academics respond to intellectual engagement, not product pitches. Strategic Relationship Value from academic contacts is high even when commercial potential is low.

*Priority:* Medium. Pursue in parallel with Priority 1–3, not at the expense of them.

---

**Priority 5 — Startup Ecosystem and Accelerators**

The Italian startup ecosystem — accelerators, incubators, innovation hubs — has direct relationships with corporate innovation managers and occasionally with HR and ESG leads through innovation partnership programs. Accelerators that work with corporate partners are a particularly efficient channel.

*Approach:* Identify accelerators and innovation programs with active corporate partner networks. Request conversations framed as "understanding how corporate partners are thinking about human impact measurement." Watch for investor stakeholders within accelerator structures — these have dual pipeline value.

*Priority:* Medium. Useful for investor pipeline in parallel with commercial pipeline. Accelerator contacts often have high Investor Interest Ratio value.

---

**Priority 6 — LinkedIn Direct Outreach**

Cold LinkedIn outreach is the lowest-conversion channel for a methodology-heavy product like KORA. It can supplement the pipeline but should not define it. The key principle: specificity converts, generality does not. A message that references a specific regulatory development, a specific challenge the stakeholder's sector faces, or a specific article the stakeholder published will always outperform a generic product description.

*Approach:* Reserve LinkedIn outreach for senior stakeholders at high-ICP-fit companies where no warm introduction path exists. Always personalize to the stakeholder's specific context. Do not include links to decks or websites in the first message. The only ask in a first LinkedIn message is a 20-minute call.

*Priority:* Low-to-medium. Use to fill gaps in the pipeline after Priority 1–5 channels are working.

---

**Priority 7 — Existing Personal Network (Non-Professional)**

Personal relationships — friends, former colleagues, family connections — can occasionally unlock access to corporate decision-makers. The risk is False Validation: personal relationships create social pressure to be polite, making weak signals look stronger than they are. Trust Score for contacts reached through personal relationships must be adjusted downward until professional credibility has been independently established.

*Approach:* Use personal network contacts to reach decision-makers, but apply strict signal quality assessment. If a personal contact offers to introduce KORA to their company but is not the budget owner and does not have organizational credibility for this topic, treat the resulting meeting as Engaged (Stage 3) at most, regardless of how enthusiastic the personal contact was.

*Priority:* Low-medium. Useful for pipeline entry; require additional signal quality verification before advancing stage.

---

### 6.2 Outreach Discipline

Across all channels, two discipline rules apply:

**Rule 1 — One channel at a time per company.** Do not pursue a company through multiple channels simultaneously. Coordinate outreach to avoid awkward overlaps that signal disorganization.

**Rule 2 — Every outreach attempt is logged in doc 14.** An outreach that receives no response is data. Pattern of non-response in a specific sector or to a specific contact type is market intelligence. Log everything.

## 7. 90-Day Validation Plan

The 90-day plan is structured into five phases, each with a clear objective, target activity, measurable KPI, and a go/no-go decision gate. The phases are sequential but overlap at the boundaries — conversations begun in Phase 1 continue through Phase 5.

---

### Phase 1 — Days 1–15: Target List Construction and First Outreach

**Objective:** Build a structured, ICP-scored target list of 50–80 companies. Launch first outreach to the highest-ICP-fit segment.

**Target activities:**
- Map existing network against ICP criteria — identify all warm introduction paths
- Build the initial Stakeholder Record list in the doc 14 intelligence layer (Stage 0 for all identified targets)
- Score initial ICP Fit hypotheses for each record using available public information (sector, size, public ESG commitments)
- Prioritize the top 20–30 records by ICP Fit Score for immediate outreach
- Contact 15–20 companies in the first two weeks through Priority 1–2 channels
- Log all outreach attempts as Stage 1 (Contacted) in the intelligence layer

**KPI target (Phase 1 exit):**
- Stakeholder Records created: ≥ 50
- Companies contacted (Stage 1): ≥ 20
- Warm introductions deployed: ≥ 10
- Responses received (Stage 2): ≥ 5

**Decision gate:**
If response rate from warm introductions is below 25% after 15 days, revisit introduction quality — the problem may be the referral framing, not the product. If ICP Fit Score cannot be populated for most targets from public information alone, the ICP definition may be too precise — broaden one dimension.

---

### Phase 2 — Days 16–30: Discovery Conversations

**Objective:** Convert responses into substantive discovery conversations. Validate problem recognition. Begin ICP empirical calibration.

**Target activities:**
- Run 10–15 discovery conversations (30–45 minutes each)
- Focus of each conversation: understand the stakeholder's current approach to impact measurement, welfare budget intelligence, ESG evidence — do NOT lead with KORA
- Allow KORA to emerge as a response to the problem the stakeholder describes
- After each conversation: update Stage, PVS, Trust Score, Objection Tags, Founder Conviction Delta in the intelligence layer
- Begin populating ICP Fit Score with empirical signal from conversations (data availability, urgency, HR maturity)
- Identify first Internal Champion candidates

**KPI target (Phase 2 exit):**
- Discovery calls completed: ≥ 10
- Stage 3 (Engaged) records: ≥ 8
- Stage 4 (Interested) records: ≥ 4
- Mean PVS across engaged records: ≥ 46
- First ICP patterns visible (at least one sector or size tier showing consistently higher PVS)
- Top three objections identified
- Founder Conviction Delta cumulative: positive

**Decision gate:**
If fewer than 4 records reach Stage 4 (Interested) after 15 discovery conversations, the primary ICP hypothesis is not confirmed — diagnose whether the problem is the audience (wrong ICP), the angle (wrong value proposition framing), or the message (wrong communication). Do not proceed to Phase 3 without understanding the cause. Adjust one variable at a time and re-test.

---

### Phase 3 — Days 31–45: MVP Demos

**Objective:** Demonstrate Foundation Light to qualified Stage 4+ stakeholders. Capture detailed value perception and pilot feasibility signals.

**Target activities:**
- Prepare a tailored Foundation Light demo for the top 5–8 Stage 4 records
- Demo structure: show the problem in the stakeholder's own language → show what KORA produces → show the methodology (briefly) → show what a pilot would look like for them specifically
- After each demo: update Stage, PVS, Trust Score, ICP Fit Score, Internal Champion Strength, Founder Conviction Delta
- Identify which demo elements produce the strongest positive signals (Feature Demand mapping)
- For Stage 5 (Pilot Consideration) transitions: log the specific trigger that moved them
- Begin identifying budget owners — ask "who inside your organization would need to be involved in a decision like this?"

**KPI target (Phase 3 exit):**
- Demos delivered: ≥ 5
- Stage 5 (Pilot Consideration) records: ≥ 3
- Stage 6 (Budget Discussion) records: ≥ 1
- Mean PVS after demo (among Stage 5+ records): ≥ 66
- Internal Champion Strength ≥ 61 on at least two records
- Feature demand patterns visible across demo feedback
- At least one company has discussed data sharing

**Decision gate:**
If demos are not advancing records past Stage 4, the gap is either the demo content (KORA is not being made concrete enough for the stakeholder's specific context) or the audience selection (demos are being delivered to interested contacts who are not pilot decision-makers). Rebuild the demo for the highest-PVS stakeholder type. Ensure budget owners are in the room before the next demo cycle.

---

### Phase 4 — Days 46–60: Pilot Qualification

**Objective:** Formally qualify the most advanced pipeline records for pilot conversion. Introduce pricing. Begin data feasibility assessment.

**Target activities:**
- For Stage 5–6 records: schedule explicit pilot scoping conversations — not demos, not discovery. These are operational conversations: what data do you have, when can you share it, who will be involved, what does success look like for you?
- Introduce indicative pricing in Stage 6 conversations — not as a negotiation, but as a signal test. A stakeholder who asks follow-up questions about pricing is a strong WTP signal. A stakeholder who goes silent after pricing is revealed needs investigation.
- Update Willingness-to-Pay Score, Budget Range Indication, and Confidence Level of Intent for all Stage 5+ records
- Assess data readiness: does the company actually have the structured data KORA needs for a Foundation Light pilot?
- Build the first Pilot Evidence Package outline for the most advanced record

**KPI target (Phase 4 exit):**
- Stage 6 (Budget Discussion) records: ≥ 2
- Stage 7 (Commitment Signal) records: ≥ 1
- WTP Score ≥ 46 on at least three records
- WTP Score ≥ 66 on at least one record
- At least one company has confirmed data availability
- Indicative pricing introduced in at least three conversations
- Top objection to pricing identified and documented

**Decision gate:**
If no records reach Stage 7 by day 60, the pipeline is stalled at qualification. The most common cause is one of three: pricing is too high for the current ICP segment (test a lower entry tier), data readiness is a universal barrier (consider a more limited first pilot scope), or internal champions have not successfully mobilized the budget owner (deepen champion strength before reintroducing pilot proposal).

---

### Phase 5 — Days 61–90: Pilot Conversion and Investor Evidence Pack

**Objective:** Convert the most advanced pipeline records to Active Validator status. Compile investor-grade evidence from the full 90-day validation dataset.

**Target activities:**
- Drive at least one record to Stage 8 (Active Validator) — formal pilot agreement, data provided, Foundation Light process initiated
- Document pilot agreement scope, data provided, and timeline for investor evidence pack
- Complete the Validation Confidence Index assessment in doc 14 — score the overall evidential quality of the 90-day dataset
- Compile investor evidence narrative: pipeline data, ICP analysis, WTP distribution, objection patterns, Active Validators, Founder Conviction Delta trend
- Identify the two or three most powerful quotes or statements from stakeholder conversations (with permission) for investor use
- Prepare a structured debrief: what was the ICP hypothesis at day 1 vs. the ICP evidence at day 90? What changed? What did the market teach KORA?
- Update all Stakeholder Records to ensure completeness before compiling the evidence pack

**KPI target (Phase 5 exit):**
- Stage 8 (Active Validator): ≥ 1 (target: 2)
- Stage 7 (Commitment Signal): ≥ 2
- Stage 6 (Budget Discussion): ≥ 3
- Total companies in pipeline: ≥ 50
- Total discovery + demo conversations: ≥ 20
- Total interactions logged: ≥ 50
- ICP defined empirically (sector + size + trigger confirmed by data)
- Top 3 objections documented with resolution approaches
- Investor evidence pack complete

**Decision gate:**
If the 90-day phase ends without any Stage 8 record, assess whether the pipeline is structurally sound but late (extend by 30 days with a specific Stage 7 record as the conversion target) or whether there is a fundamental positioning or product-market fit issue requiring a strategy reset. Do not treat a busy but stagnant pipeline as validation — stage depth, not stage breadth, is the evidence standard.

## 9. Investor-Grade Evidence

Investors evaluating KORA's traction will ask, directly or implicitly: "How do you know the market wants this?" The answer must come from structured data, not storytelling. This section defines the evidence standard and the evidence components that the 90-day validation phase must produce.

### 9.1 What Investors Actually Want to See

Sophisticated investors in early-stage B2B products assess four things:

1. **The problem is real and recognized.** Not by the founder — by target customers, in their own words.
2. **A definable customer profile exists.** Not "any company with employees" — a specific, testable ICP with observable characteristics.
3. **The commercial pathway is credible.** Not just that people liked the idea — that someone with budget authority discussed paying for it.
4. **The team is learning systematically.** The validation approach itself signals whether the team is capable of building a scalable commercial process.

### 9.2 Evidence Components

**Component 1 — Conversion Funnel Data**
A fully populated pipeline with stage counts, conversion rates, and time-to-stage metrics. This demonstrates that KORA's commercial process is systematic and measurable. A funnel with 50 companies at Stage 1 and 5 at Stage 4 tells a different story than 50 companies at Stage 1 and 0 at Stage 4. Both are honest. The funnel data itself is the evidence.

**Component 2 — ICP Analysis**
A data-grounded description of the company profile most associated with high PVS, stage progression, and WTP signals. Includes ICP Fit Score distribution analysis and sector-level Interest Density. This answers: "Who specifically is buying this?"

**Component 3 — Willingness-to-Pay Signals**
A structured, non-binding mapping of budget signals across the pipeline. At least three records with WTP Score ≥ 46 and Confidence Level of Intent ≥ Medium. At least one record with WTP Score ≥ 66. This answers: "Is there commercial demand, or just interest?"

**Component 4 — Pilot Pipeline**
At least one Stage 8 (Active Validator) record with documented pilot scope and data provision. At least two Stage 7 (Commitment Signal) records with documented intent and next step. This answers: "Are companies actually testing the product?"

**Component 5 — Objection Analysis**
A structured Objection Frequency Map showing the top five objections, their sector distribution, and the resolution approaches developed. This answers: "What are the barriers, and are you addressing them?"

**Component 6 — Founder Learning Evidence**
A Founder Conviction Delta trend showing a positive cumulative trajectory — evidence that the market is confirming rather than undermining the KORA thesis. This answers: "Is the team's belief in this grounded in reality, or in wishful thinking?"

**Component 7 — Testimonials and Quotes**
With explicit permission: two to five direct quotes from senior stakeholders — CHROs, CFOs, ESG leads, CEOs — that describe the problem in their own language and reference KORA as a credible response. Quotes from budget owners are worth ten times quotes from individual contributors. Attribution level (named / role only / anonymous) is negotiated case by case.

**Component 8 — Repeatable Use Case**
At least one clearly defined use case — a company type, a trigger event, a workflow — where KORA's value can be described with sufficient specificity that it is recognizable to other companies in the same situation. The repeatable use case is the seed of KORA's commercial narrative.

**Component 9 — Clear Wedge**
A crisp one- or two-sentence statement of what KORA does that no other product does — grounded in market feedback, not founder assertion. The wedge must have been confirmed by at least three Stage 4+ stakeholders who said, in their own words, that this specific capability was not available elsewhere.

## 10. False Validation Risks

The most dangerous outcome of the 90-day validation phase is not failure — it is the appearance of success built on false signals. False validation wastes the next six months building a product no one will pay for or pursuing customers who will never convert. These risks must be actively managed.

### 10.1 Politeness Masquerading as Interest

**The risk:** Most people are polite. A senior executive who takes a 30-minute meeting, says "this is really interesting," and agrees to "stay in touch" has given the founder nothing except Stage 2 data (Responded). The founder who interprets this as validation has made a dangerous error.

**The signal test:** Did the stakeholder take any action before the meeting ended — a follow-up request, a colleague introduction, a data question? If no action was taken and no specific next step was agreed, the interaction is a weak signal at most.

**The response:** Log accurately in doc 14. Do not advance the Stage. Do not increase PVS above 25. Set a specific follow-up date. If three follow-ups produce no action, move to Stage 9.

---

### 10.2 Friends and Network Validating the Vision

**The risk:** The founder's personal and professional network is predisposed to support. Enthusiastic responses from friends, former colleagues, and mutual connections — especially at early networking events — systematically overstate demand. They are validating the founder's passion and relationship, not the product.

**The signal test:** Does the person have a real problem that KORA solves? Do they have budget authority or organizational mandate for this type of initiative? Would they be professionally embarrassed if KORA failed to deliver?

**The response:** Lower Trust Score automatically for contacts reached through personal relationships. Only raise Trust Score when professional credibility has been independently established. Treat Stage 3 from personal contacts as equivalent to Stage 2 from professional contacts.

---

### 10.3 No Budget Owner Involved

**The risk:** Conversations progress through multiple meetings with an engaged CHRO or ESG lead who genuinely values KORA — but the budget owner has never been part of the conversation. The CHRO moves KORA forward on their own but hits a wall when they need to secure funding.

**The signal test:** Has the budget owner been named? Have they been introduced? Has any conversation with the stakeholder referenced how they would secure internal funding for this initiative?

**The response:** Explicitly ask, at Stage 4 or later: "Who would be involved in a decision to move forward with this?" If the answer is "just me," probe further — HR directors rarely have unilateral budget authority for vendor decisions. If the budget owner cannot be identified by Stage 5, the Internal Champion Strength score should reflect this gap.

---

### 10.4 Data Sharing Resistance

**The risk:** Companies express strong interest in KORA's outputs but are unwilling to provide the data that Foundation Light requires. An interested company that refuses data sharing cannot be an Active Validator. Data sharing is the minimum operational commitment for Foundation Light.

**The signal test:** Has data sharing been explicitly discussed? Has the stakeholder identified the data sources available? Has a privacy or legal concern been raised, and has it been addressed?

**The response:** At Stage 5 (Pilot Consideration), data availability must be assessed. A company that has not discussed data sharing by Stage 5 is not genuinely considering a pilot — it is exploring KORA as a concept. Raise the data question directly. A negative response is a Stage 9 signal or a major ICP fit revision.

---

### 10.5 No Urgency

**The risk:** Companies recognize the problem, like KORA's solution, but have no near-term trigger that makes action necessary now. "We'll definitely revisit this next year" is a polite exit, not a commitment.

**The signal test:** Is there a specific deadline, event, regulatory obligation, or internal decision cycle that makes this problem urgent in the next three to six months? Has the stakeholder referenced it explicitly?

**The response:** Urgency is an ICP component. If a company has high ICP Fit on all other dimensions but low urgency, it belongs in the pipeline but not in the active conversion priority. Flag in the ICP Fit Score, note in record rationale, and set a follow-up for the moment when urgency is likely to increase (ESG audit season, budget cycle, CSRD deadline).

---

### 10.6 Free Pilots Only

**The risk:** Companies are willing to "try it" for free but have never discussed budget. Free pilots without a defined commercial pathway are expensive for KORA (resource investment) and worthless for commercial validation. They produce case study material but not WTP evidence.

**The signal test:** Has pricing been mentioned? Has the stakeholder indicated any budget could be associated with this? Is there an agreed path from pilot to commercial agreement?

**The response:** Never offer a free pilot without a defined next step. Structure the pilot proposal as: "Foundation Light pilot at [indicative price range], which we're offering at [discounted/structured introductory rate] for the first cohort of validation partners, with [specific deliverable] at the end." A company unwilling to pay anything — even a nominal amount — for a structured pilot is not a paying customer. It may be a case study partner, which has different (and lower) commercial value.

---

### 10.7 Too Many Different Customer Types

**The risk:** The pipeline grows with companies from many different sectors, sizes, and maturity levels — each generating interesting conversations but no repeatable pattern. The ICP remains undefined. Investors see a diffuse pipeline; the team chases every promising conversation without systematic prioritization.

**The signal test:** After 30 discovery conversations, can the founder describe a specific company profile that is meaningfully more receptive than others? If not, the ICP is diffuse.

**The response:** At the Phase 2 exit gate (Day 30), if the ICP Fit Distribution shows no sector or size concentration in the Stage 4+ records, deliberately narrow outreach to the two highest-ICP-fit segments and deprioritize others temporarily. A narrow, deep pipeline in two segments is more valuable for validation than a wide, shallow pipeline across ten.

---

### 10.8 Confusing Vision Interest with Product Interest

**The risk:** KORA's vision — making human value visible and measurable — is genuinely compelling to many people. Vision interest is not product interest. A stakeholder who loves the idea of impact intelligence may have no intention of, or mandate for, actually implementing Foundation Light.

**The signal test:** Has the conversation moved from "this is an important problem" to "this is how we would use it, and here is our specific context"? Has the stakeholder asked product-specific questions — about data formats, about the methodology, about what the output would look like for their company specifically?

**The response:** Vision interest is Stage 3 (Engaged) at most. To advance to Stage 4 (Interested), the stakeholder must have connected KORA specifically to their own organizational reality. Vision conversations with no contextual grounding are noted as positive Founder Conviction Delta signals but not advanced in pipeline stage.

## 11. Founder Risk Log

A founder who cannot articulate what may invalidate the thesis is not validating honestly — they are confirming it. The Founder Risk Log is not a pessimism exercise. It is the structural prerequisite for honest validation. Every risk that is named and tracked can be monitored, responded to, and potentially resolved. Every risk that remains unnamed will surface anyway — but later, at higher cost, after more time has been invested in the wrong direction.

**Governing principle:** Risks are not failures. Unmeasured risks are failures.

The following risk categories define the landscape of threats to KORA's validation thesis during the 90-day MVP validation phase. For each risk, the log defines: what the risk is, what evidence would support it, what evidence would weaken it, early warning indicators, and mitigation approach.

### 11.A Market Risks

---

**Risk A1 — Companies Love the Vision But Not the First Product**

*Definition:* Stakeholders enthusiastically engage with KORA's vision of human impact intelligence but show no interest in Foundation Light as a concrete, purchasable product. They want the future state, not the current offering.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Conversations consistently reach Stage 3–4 (Engaged / Interested) but never advance to Stage 5 (Pilot Consideration). PVS rises during vision conversations but falls when Foundation Light specifics are discussed. Stakeholders say "call me when you have X" (a feature not in Foundation Light). No records cross the data-sharing discussion threshold.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least three records advance to Stage 5 after a concrete Foundation Light demo. Stakeholders ask practical implementation questions (data formats, timeline, scope) rather than conceptual questions. Willingness-to-Pay signals appear before pilot commitment is raised.

*Early warning indicators:* Consistent high PVS but persistent zero WTP; demo conversations that generate enthusiasm but no operational follow-through; first pilot qualification conversations that stall immediately on product scope.

*Mitigation:* Tighten the Foundation Light demo to show a specific, realistic output for the stakeholder's actual company profile. Reduce the vision section and increase the "here is what you get on day 30" section. Test whether a more limited, more concrete pilot offer (a narrow diagnostic of one data source only) unlocks operational commitment.

---

**Risk A2 — Inability to Define a Repeatable ICP**

*Definition:* After 20+ discovery conversations, no sector, size tier, or company profile shows consistently higher conversion rates, PVS, or WTP signals than others. The market appears interested but diffuse. No repeatable customer type emerges.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* ICP Fit Distribution after 20 conversations shows no cluster above 65. Stage 4+ records are distributed across five or more distinct sectors with no concentration. WTP signals appear randomly, not in a pattern.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* Two or more sectors show Interest Density above 35%. Company size tier M or L consistently produces higher PVS and stage progression. At least one repeatable urgency trigger (CSRD, board pressure, welfare budget review) produces consistent ICP signals.

*Early warning indicators:* Founder unable to name the target customer type with confidence at the Phase 2 exit gate (Day 30). ICP Fit Score component scoring shows high variance across records in all dimensions.

*Mitigation:* Deliberately narrow outreach to the two highest-ICP-fit companies already in the pipeline. Build two deep cases instead of many shallow ones. Use the discovery from those cases to re-define ICP criteria empirically, then re-expand.

---

**Risk A3 — Market Is Too Early for the Category**

*Definition:* The "human impact intelligence" category is not yet recognized as a need by a sufficient proportion of target buyers. The market is open to the vision but has not yet felt the urgency that drives purchasing decisions.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* CSRD references appear in fewer than 20% of conversations with eligible companies. Welfare budget ROI questions appear in fewer than 15% of CFO conversations. Urgency indicators (Section 4.6) are consistently below target frequency. "We'll need this eventually" language appears frequently.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* CSRD pressure is referenced unprompted in 40%+ of conversations. Outcome-metric language appears in 30%+ of HR leader conversations. Three or more companies are in an active regulatory or board-reporting cycle that requires what KORA produces.

*Early warning indicators:* All conversations reach Stage 3–4 but no company moves to Stage 5 despite strong PVS. The missing element in every conversion is urgency, not interest.

*Mitigation:* Test a more specific urgency framing: instead of leading with KORA's full value proposition, open with the specific regulatory or board pressure trigger that makes KORA necessary now. If this framing accelerates conversion in even two or three conversations, the product is correctly timed — the entry message was wrong, not the market timing.

### 11.B Product Risks

---

**Risk B1 — KORA Perceived as Too Complex**

*Definition:* Stakeholders understand the problem KORA solves but find the Foundation Light offering difficult to grasp, operationalize, or explain internally. Complexity becomes the primary barrier to adoption.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Multiple stakeholders ask for simplification after demos. Internal champions struggle to describe KORA to colleagues. The most common objection after demos is "this seems complex to implement" or "we'd need IT involved" despite Foundation Light requiring no integration. Decision cycles extend because internal explanation fails.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* CHROs successfully introduce KORA to CFOs or CEOs using their own language after one meeting with the founder. Stakeholders accurately describe KORA's value proposition in follow-up emails. Demo-to-Stage 5 conversion happens in one session.

*Early warning indicators:* Internal Champion Strength fails to increase after demos (the champion cannot mobilize internally). Stage 5+ conversations frequently get stuck on "let me explain this to our team" without a next step date.

*Mitigation:* Redesign the demo to use the stakeholder's own language and their own specific data examples. Develop a one-page "what KORA does for [company type]" summary that champions can use internally without the founder in the room. Test whether a smaller, more bounded pilot scope reduces perceived complexity.

---

**Risk B2 — Data Availability Is a Universal Barrier**

*Definition:* Target companies systematically lack the data quality, structure, or accessibility required for Foundation Light to produce meaningful outputs. Data readiness is lower than the ICP definition assumed.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Data readiness questions in pilot qualification conversations (Phase 4) consistently reveal that companies cannot provide structured exports from welfare platforms, HR systems, or ESG records without significant internal effort. Three or more records that advanced to Stage 5 stall at data feasibility assessment.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least two companies confirm data availability in structured or semi-structured form within one conversation. Foundation Light's ingestion model (designed for messy, heterogeneous data) handles real data samples from at least two companies without major redesign.

*Early warning indicators:* Data readiness component of ICP Fit Score consistently scores below 40 across the pipeline. Stage 5 conversations produce enthusiasm but a consistent inability to name a specific data source for pilot ingestion.

*Mitigation:* Reduce the minimum data bar for a pilot to the single most accessible data source (welfare platform export only, or HR records only). Build a diagnostic that shows meaningful output even from one data source. Position Foundation Light even more explicitly as a "work with what you have" product. Update the ICP definition to weight data readiness more heavily.

---

**Risk B3 — Implementation Friction Too High**

*Definition:* Even where interest and data readiness are confirmed, the operational process of completing a Foundation Light pilot creates friction that causes deals to stall or fail to deliver value in the committed timeline.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Active Validators experience delays in data provision, internal coordination, or output review that extend the pilot far beyond the agreed timeline. First pilot outputs fail to meet stakeholder expectations. Pilot completion requires more founder time than was anticipated.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* The first pilot completes within the committed timeline with clear outputs and a meaningful executive presentation. The founder's time investment per pilot is bounded and scalable. Stakeholders report that the onboarding and data process was simpler than expected.

*Early warning indicators:* Data provision delays exceed two weeks from pilot agreement. Stakeholders request custom analyses outside the Foundation Light scope. Pilot outputs generate confusion rather than clarity.

*Mitigation:* Define a rigid pilot scope in advance: specific data sources, specific output format, specific timeline, specific success definition (see Section 12). Do not expand scope after agreement. Build a simple, replicable pilot onboarding checklist that covers all steps from data request to executive delivery.

### 11.C Commercial Risks

---

**Risk C1 — Validation Conversations Never Progress to Budget**

*Definition:* The pipeline advances to Stage 4–5 consistently but stalls before budget is ever mentioned. No WTP signals emerge. Companies are interested but the commercial conversation never opens.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* WTP Score remains at 0 across all pipeline records after 45 days. Budget Range Indication field unpopulated across all records. Pricing has been raised in at least five conversations but produced topic-avoidance each time.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least two records generate WTP Score ≥ 46 within the first 45 days. Budget is mentioned spontaneously by at least one stakeholder before the founder raises it.

*Early warning indicators:* Internal Champion Strength stays below 40 across the pipeline (no one has the organizational mandate to initiate a budget conversation). Budget owners are consistently absent from advanced-stage conversations.

*Mitigation:* Earlier and more explicit budget owner inclusion: at Stage 4, ask specifically who controls budget for this type of initiative. Restructure the pilot proposal to make the commercial question explicit and unavoidable. Test a very low-cost pilot entry point to lower the budget conversation threshold.

---

**Risk C2 — Too Many Custom Requests from Early Companies**

*Definition:* Early stakeholders are interested in KORA but only if it is customized to their specific needs — data formats, sector-specific outputs, integration with specific systems, or methodology variations. Each pilot becomes bespoke, preventing scalability.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Pilot scoping conversations consistently produce lists of custom requirements that are outside Foundation Light scope. Active Validators require significant founder time on non-replicable customization. No two pilots have the same structure.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least two pilots can be completed with substantially the same methodology, data process, and output format. Stakeholders accept Foundation Light's standard output without requesting customization in the scope-setting conversation.

*Early warning indicators:* Phase 4 scoping conversations extend beyond two sessions. Custom requirement lists appear in two or more consecutive pilot discussions.

*Mitigation:* Establish and defend a clear Foundation Light product boundary at the start of every pilot scoping conversation: "Here is what Foundation Light includes. Here is what it does not include. The pilot covers only this scope." Customization requests beyond scope are documented as future product signals (Feature Demand), not accepted as pilot scope changes.

---

**Risk C3 — Free Pilots Crowd Out Paid Engagement**

*Definition:* The market is willing to engage with KORA as a free or highly discounted pilot but resists paying even nominal amounts. The result is a crowded pipeline of non-paying validators that consumes founder time without generating revenue or WTP evidence.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Three or more "pilot agreements" are reached where no budget has been discussed and no payment has been made. Stakeholders consistently reframe the conversation away from commercial terms when pricing is introduced.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least one company commits to a paid pilot (any amount) within the first 60 days. At least two WTP Score ≥ 66 records exist in the pipeline by Day 60.

*Early warning indicators:* Willingness-to-Pay Score ≤ 25 across all Stage 7 records. Budget Range Indication = NO_SIGNAL across all Stage 6–7 records.

*Mitigation:* Never advance a record past Stage 7 without at least a preliminary budget conversation. Reframe "free pilot" offers as "introductory rate" with a defined commercial pathway. Minimum commitment signal for Stage 8: either data has been provided OR a nominal commercial commitment has been made.

### 11.D Timing Risks

---

**Risk D1 — Long B2B Cycles Creating False Optimism**

*Definition:* B2B sales cycles in the mid-market HR/ESG space routinely run six to eighteen months. The pipeline may appear healthy during the 90-day validation phase — with many companies at Stage 3–5 — while the actual signal is that no company will convert in a commercially meaningful timeframe.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* All Stage 5+ records have introduced internal complexity that extends conversion timelines beyond 90 days. No company is on a trajectory to reach Stage 8 within the validation period. Stakeholders consistently reference procurement cycles, budget freezes, or internal approval timelines of six months or more.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least one record reaches Stage 8 within 60 days. Two or more records are in active Stage 6–7 with a specific decision date within 30 days. At least one company has self-initiated the acceleration of its internal process.

*Early warning indicators:* Every Stage 5+ record has a "next step" date more than 45 days in the future. Stakeholders routinely defer with "we need to wait for [internal cycle]." No record has advanced two stages within the same month.

*Mitigation:* Create specific urgency catalysts: time-limited introductory pricing, cohort-based pilot structuring ("we're onboarding three companies in Q[X] — would [Company] like to be among them?"), or regulatory deadline anchoring. Prioritize companies with near-term triggers. Measure and report conversion velocity (days per stage) as a leading indicator of cycle compression.

---

**Risk D2 — Budget Freeze or Procurement Cycle Timing**

*Definition:* Target companies have genuine interest and budget in principle, but the budget cycle for the current year is already committed, procurement processes require long lead times, or HR budgets are frozen due to broader cost-reduction initiatives.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Two or more Stage 6 conversations stall with "budget is committed for this year" or "this is a next-year conversation." No Stage 7 record exists after 60 days despite high PVS and strong ICP fit.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least one company accesses discretionary budget for a pilot outside the standard procurement cycle. A Stage 6 company produces a signed pilot agreement within the validation window.

*Early warning indicators:* Budget Range Indication = NO_SIGNAL on all records above Stage 5. WTP Score high but "timing" objection is the primary blocker for all Stage 5+ records.

*Mitigation:* Map procurement cycles in ICP refinement: companies closing fiscal year in March are in planning cycle in October–December. Target companies with near-term budget openness. Develop a micro-pilot offer at a price point that qualifies as discretionary spend without formal procurement.

### 11.E Methodology Risks

---

**Risk E1 — Methodology Perceived as Opaque or Unjustified**

*Definition:* Sophisticated stakeholders — especially CFOs, legal leads, and ESG auditors — challenge KORA's methodology as arbitrary, unvalidated, or difficult to defend. The classification system, weighting logic, or index construction appears subjective.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Two or more conversations produce methodological objections from credible stakeholders. Legal or compliance leads request external certification or peer-reviewed validation before proceeding. An ESG auditor expresses concern about the defensibility of KORA's outputs in an audit context.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* Methodology documentation successfully addresses at least three skeptical stakeholder reviews without modifications. ESG leads accept the methodology versioning and auditability architecture as sufficient for reporting purposes. At least one legal lead expresses no methodological concerns after review.

*Early warning indicators:* "Methodology Unfamiliarity" objection appears in more than three records. Stage 5+ conversations stall specifically on methodology questions, not on data or budget.

*Mitigation:* Prepare a methodology brief (not a full whitepaper — a precise three-page document) that explains the canonical IU formula, pillar classification logic, and KORA Index construction in plain language accessible to non-experts. Anticipate the "how do you know this is right" question with a structured answer: "The methodology is versioned, auditable, and designed to make its assumptions explicit — not to hide them." For advanced methodological challenges, reference the StressTest (Appendix A) as numerical validation.

### 11.F Trust and Privacy Risks

---

**Risk F1 — Organizations Refuse Data Sharing on Privacy Grounds**

*Definition:* Target companies are interested in KORA but refuse to share employee data — even in aggregated or pseudonymized form — due to GDPR concerns, employee trust concerns, or legal advice. Data sharing resistance blocks every potential pilot.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Three or more Stage 5 conversations stall specifically at the data sharing discussion. Legal or HR leads express GDPR-based refusal to share any employee data with a third party. Two or more pilot discussions collapse immediately when data formats are discussed.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least two companies confirm that welfare platform exports or HR aggregate reports are shareable under existing data processing arrangements. KORA's pseudonymization architecture, when explained, resolves privacy concerns in at least two conversations.

*Early warning indicators:* "Privacy Concern" objection appears in more than 25% of Stage 4+ records. Data readiness discussions in Stage 5 consistently identify privacy as the blocker rather than data availability.

*Mitigation:* Prepare a clear one-page privacy architecture brief explaining KORA's data model: only aggregate or pseudonymized inputs required, no individual identification possible, employer never sees individual-level scores. Have a Data Processing Agreement template available at Stage 5. Position the privacy architecture as a feature, not a compliance afterthought — which it is.

**Risk F2 — Welfare Providers or HR Platforms Resist Data Access**

*Definition:* Companies want to pilot KORA but the welfare management platforms or HR systems that hold the relevant data do not provide accessible data exports, or charge for export access, or actively resist integration with third-party intelligence tools.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Data readiness discussions reveal that welfare platform exports are not accessible in the agreed format. Two or more potential pilots stall because the welfare platform does not produce clean, exportable data. A welfare provider specifically objects to its data being analyzed by KORA.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least two welfare platforms provide clean CSV or structured exports that KORA can ingest. Welfare providers do not raise objections to data export when the pilot is framed as analytics-on-top, not as a competing service.

*Early warning indicators:* Data readiness component of ICP Fit Score is consistently low even in companies with stated data availability. Stage 5 data feasibility conversations reveal platform-specific barriers that were not anticipated.

*Mitigation:* Map which welfare platforms and HR systems are most common in the Italian mid-market ICP. Build import support and data format guidance for the top five. Pre-test data ingestion feasibility before finalizing pilot agreements. Treat platform data access as an ICP dimension.

### 11.G Founder Risks

---

**Risk G1 — Founder Over-Interpreting Weak Signals**

*Definition:* The founder — through optimism bias, relationship pressure, or the natural desire for validation — systematically interprets weak signals as moderate or strong signals, inflating the pipeline and distorting the evidence picture.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Founder Conviction Delta is consistently positive (+3 to +5) across nearly all interactions, including interactions where stage did not advance. PVS scores are consistently high (70+) across records where WTP remains at zero. Multiple records are in Stage 4–5 but have had no substantive interaction in 30+ days.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* Founder Conviction Delta shows genuine variation — including negative and neutral values — across different stakeholder types and conversations. Stage advancement is correlated with PVS increases, not just with meetings scheduled. At least 20% of records are at Stage 9 (Lost) by Day 60.

*Early warning indicators:* Zero negative Founder Conviction Delta values in 20+ interactions. Stage 9 records represent less than 10% of the pipeline despite active outreach. The pipeline has grown but the ratio of Stage 4+ records to total records has declined.

*Mitigation:* Commit to applying the signal strength framework (Section 2) rigorously and consistently, including the downgrading of personal-network and politeness signals. Review the Founder Conviction Delta log weekly and explicitly challenge any sequence of uniformly positive deltas. Ask a trusted advisor — who has not met the stakeholders — to independently review the top five pipeline records and assess signal quality. External calibration of internal optimism is a structural requirement, not a sign of weakness.

---

**Risk G2 — Founder-Dependence of Early Validation**

*Definition:* Early validation conversations are driven entirely by the founder's personal credibility, network, and ability to explain KORA. This produces results that cannot be replicated by a commercial team and does not demonstrate market demand independent of the founder's presence.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* All Stage 4+ records were generated through the founder's direct network. No stakeholder has proactively referred KORA to another company without being asked. When the founder has been absent from a follow-up meeting, progress has stalled.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* At least one stakeholder has introduced KORA to a second company without being asked. At least two records were generated through non-founder channels (advisor introduction, consultant referral, inbound inquiry). Internal champions sustain conversations without the founder in the room.

*Early warning indicators:* All pipeline entries trace back to the founder's direct first-degree connections. No record has advanced stages without the founder personally present in the meeting.

*Mitigation:* Deliberately generate non-founder-initiated meetings from Day 30 onward. Invest in Internal Champion Strength in Stage 5+ records so that champions can advance the process without the founder. Test whether a prepared demo deck can produce stage advancement without the founder presenting it. These are leading indicators of future scalability.

### 11.H Execution Risks

---

**Risk H1 — Pilot Execution Fails to Deliver Value**

*Definition:* The first Foundation Light pilot is completed technically — data ingested, outputs generated — but the outputs fail to meet stakeholder expectations, produce meaningful insights, or generate a follow-on commercial conversation. The pilot succeeds technically but fails strategically.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* Active Validator stakeholder does not engage meaningfully with KORA outputs. Executive presentation generates polite acknowledgment but no follow-on discussion. The pilot delivers a KORA Index but the stakeholder cannot use it for any internal decision or external reporting.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* Pilot outputs generate specific internal conversations the company had not been able to have before ("I didn't know this pillar was underinvested"). Stakeholder requests a second analysis cycle or an expansion of scope. The pilot output is referenced in an internal or external report.

*Early warning indicators:* Active Validator goes quiet after receiving outputs. No follow-up meeting is scheduled within two weeks of output delivery. Stakeholder requests significant modifications to the output format after the first delivery.

*Mitigation:* Define success criteria for the pilot in advance, jointly with the stakeholder (see Section 12 — Pilot Success Definition Framework). Structure the output delivery as an interactive session, not a document handoff. Build a post-pilot review ritual: one hour with the key stakeholder to review outputs, identify insights, and define next steps. If the pilot does not open a commercial follow-on conversation, document what specifically was missing and treat it as a product failure to learn from, not just a sales failure.

---

**Risk H2 — Insufficient Bandwidth for Simultaneous Pilots**

*Definition:* Multiple stakeholders enter Stage 8 (Active Validator) simultaneously, creating a resource constraint that prevents any pilot from being executed with the quality and focus it requires. Pilot quality declines across all engagements.

*Evidence supporting this risk:* More than two Active Validators simultaneously create scheduling conflicts. Pilot timelines extend due to divided founder attention. Output quality for at least one pilot is lower than it would have been with focused attention.

*Evidence weakening this risk:* The pilot process is sufficiently systematized that three simultaneous pilots can be managed without quality degradation. A defined pilot workflow allows delegation of data processing to a team member or tool.

*Early warning indicators:* Stage 7 record count reaches three or more simultaneously. Pilot scoping conversations identify divergent timelines that would require simultaneous execution.

*Mitigation:* Define a maximum simultaneous pilot count (suggested: two for Foundation Light MVP phase). Use this limit as a commercial signal — "we have one slot available in Q[X] for a validation partner" — rather than as an operational constraint to apologize for. A limited pilot cohort creates appropriate scarcity.

## 12. Pilot Success Definition Framework

Without a predefined success framework, pilots generate activity but not evidence. A pilot that ends with "it was interesting" is not a success — it is an expensive conversation. A pilot that produces no follow-on commercial discussion, no case study opportunity, and no investor-reportable signal has consumed significant founder time and delivered nothing.

Success for a Foundation Light pilot must be defined jointly with the stakeholder before the pilot begins, and evaluated rigorously against that definition at completion. The evaluation must then determine: what level of success was achieved, what does that imply for the commercial relationship, and what is the recommended next action.

---

### 12.A Minimum Technical Success

Technical success is the prerequisite for all other success levels. It means the Foundation Light process completed and produced outputs.

**Criteria:**
- Data received from the stakeholder company in an agreed format
- Data successfully ingested and mapped to KORA's classification framework
- All five KORA pillars (LIFE, GROWTH, CONNECTION, IMPACT, LEGACY) have at least one record classified
- KORA Index produced with at least three of ten components populated with real data
- Confidence scores applied to all data sources (reflecting actual data quality, not inflated)
- Methodology traceability preserved: every output can be traced to a specific classified action and a specific IU calculation
- Outputs delivered to the agreed timeline

**Failure mode:** Technical success without organizational success is a production success with no strategic value. Do not report technical success as pilot success to investors.

---

### 12.B Minimum Organizational Success

Organizational success means the stakeholder organization engaged with the outputs and the pilot produced internal value beyond the founder's time investment.

**Criteria:**
- At least one structured stakeholder review session has occurred (not just report delivery)
- An executive or board-level presentation of outputs has been prepared and delivered
- At least one internal stakeholder who was not part of the original project team has reviewed or discussed the outputs
- The stakeholder company understands what the KORA outputs mean in the context of their programs
- At least one insight or finding from the pilot has generated an internal discussion that would not have happened without KORA
- The stakeholder expresses a desire for a follow-up analysis or next cycle

**Failure mode:** Organizational success without commercial success is the "free research" trap. The company learned something valuable. KORA learned nothing commercial and generated no revenue signal.

---

### 12.C Minimum Commercial Success

Commercial success means the pilot has produced a clear, concrete signal of willingness to pay — either through a confirmed next-phase discussion or through an explicit budget indication.

**Criteria:**
- At least one substantive conversation about next steps has occurred — not "we'll be in touch" but a specific discussion of what a continued engagement would look like
- The stakeholder has referenced budget in the context of a continued KORA engagement (even informally)
- Willingness-to-Pay Score has been updated to ≥ 46 (Moderate or above) based on post-pilot signals
- A renewal, extension, or next-tier engagement is actively under discussion
- At least one additional KORA module or capability has been requested

**Failure mode:** A pilot that ends without a commercial discussion is not a business development activity — it is market research. It may have Strategic Success value (case study, reference, investor signal), but it must not be counted as commercial validation.

---

### 12.D Strategic Success

Strategic success captures value created beyond the direct commercial relationship. A pilot can have high strategic success even if commercial success is partial or absent.

**Criteria:**
- The stakeholder company grants permission for KORA to use the pilot as a case study (named or anonymized)
- The stakeholder provides a testimonial quotation for investor and commercial use
- The stakeholder refers KORA to at least one other company or contact unprompted
- The pilot outcome, when described to investors or advisors, generates material interest or reinforcement
- The pilot gives KORA a credible foothold in a priority sector or with a brand-credible company
- The pilot has been referenced by an advisor or accelerator contact as evidence of KORA's real-world viability

---

### 12.E Pilot Success Levels

| Success Level | Definition | Implications |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical Only** | Data ingested, outputs produced. No organizational, commercial, or strategic value demonstrated. | Not a successful pilot. Diagnose: was the output wrong, or was organizational onboarding insufficient? Do not count toward investor traction evidence. |
| **Partial Success** | Technical + Organizational criteria met. No commercial or strategic signal. | Valuable learning. Provides case study material if permission obtained. Does not constitute commercial validation. Extend relationship but diagnose commercial gap before next pilot. |
| **Operational Success** | Technical + Organizational + Commercial criteria met. Clear next-step discussion and WTP signal. | Valid commercial validation evidence. Count toward investor traction. Prioritize conversion to Foundation tier or follow-on engagement. |
| **Strategic Success** | Operational criteria met + high Strategic Success (case study, testimonial, referrals, investor signal). | Highest-value outcome. Prioritize as investor evidence and reference case. May also be the anchor of a sector narrative. |
| **Full Validation Success** | All criteria met. Paid pilot or LOI confirmed. Case study permission. Referral generated. | Milestone-level outcome. Primary investor evidence. Announce internally and in investor communications. |

### 12.F How to Use the Pilot Success Framework

**Before the pilot begins:** Define the success criteria jointly with the stakeholder. For organizational success: "What insight would make this valuable for your team?" For commercial success: "If the analysis produces what we're expecting, what would the natural next conversation be?" For strategic success: "Would you be open to using this as a reference case if it delivers what we hope?"

**During the pilot:** Track against criteria as the work proceeds. Do not wait until delivery to assess. If technical criteria cannot be met (data issues, scope creep, timeline delay), escalate immediately — do not deliver partial outputs and call them a pilot.

**At pilot completion:** Conduct a formal evaluation against all criteria. Document the result as one of the five success levels. Update the Stakeholder Record in doc 14 with: pilot outcome, success level reached, follow-on discussion status, case study permission, testimonial status, referrals generated.

**For investor use:** Only Operational Success, Strategic Success, or Full Validation Success count as investor-reportable traction. "We ran a pilot" is not an investor signal. "We completed a pilot that reached [Operational / Strategic / Full Validation] success level" is.

**A failed pilot with strong learning may still be strategically valuable** — if the failure clarifies a specific product, ICP, or positioning gap that can be addressed. Document and analyze. A technically successful pilot with no commercial signal is not full validation — it is an expensive demo that needs to be diagnosed.

## 13. Decision Rules

These rules define when to take specific actions in the validation pipeline. They eliminate ambiguity and prevent the most common failure modes: over-investing in low-signal relationships, under-investing in high-signal ones, and misjudging when to move a conversation from exploration to commitment.

### 13.1 When to Continue Pursuing a Company

Continue active pursuit when:
- Engagement Stage ≥ 3 AND at least one conversation has occurred in the last 21 days
- Perceived Value Score ≥ 46 AND Trust Score ≥ 41 (signal quality is sufficient)
- A specific next action has been agreed and is dated
- ICP Fit Score ≥ 50 (structurally a plausible customer)

Do not continue active pursuit solely because a relationship feels warm. Apply the signal criteria.

### 13.2 When to Deprioritize

Deprioritize (reduce active contact frequency; move to low-touch monitoring) when:
- No response to three follow-up contacts within 45 days
- PVS remains below 25 after two substantive conversations
- ICP Fit Score below 40 after full discovery conversation
- No budget owner has been identified or accessible by Stage 5
- Stakeholder explicitly references a competing tool and shows no interest in understanding differentiation
- Founder Conviction Delta from all interactions with this company is negative

Deprioritized records remain in the pipeline and are not moved to Stage 9 unless rejection is explicit. They remain available for re-engagement if market context changes.

### 13.3 When to Request Data Sharing

Request a data sharing discussion when:
- Stakeholder has reached Stage 5 (Pilot Consideration) — they are exploring feasibility, which requires understanding data availability
- OR PVS ≥ 66 AND at least two meetings have occurred — strong enough signal to test operational commitment

Frame the data discussion as: "To give you a realistic picture of what Foundation Light would produce for your company, we'd need to understand what data you have. Can we spend 20 minutes looking at what's available?" This is not a commitment ask — it is a feasibility conversation.

Never request data sharing before Stage 4. It signals operational seriousness before trust and interest have been established.

### 13.4 When to Discuss Price

Discuss pricing when:
- Stakeholder is at Stage 5 or Stage 6 — feasibility is being explored or budget is in scope
- Budget owner has been introduced to the conversation
- The stakeholder has asked "what would this cost?" (the strongest possible invitation)
- OR the conversation has reached a natural conclusion point where next steps require a commercial framing

Never volunteer pricing in a discovery or demo meeting that has not yet surfaced genuine interest. A premature price conversation collapses trust before value has been established. When asked about price before Stage 5, give a range rather than a specific number: "Foundation Light for a company your size is typically in the range of [X–Y]. We'd define the exact scope in a pilot scoping session."

### 13.5 When to Propose a Pilot

Propose a formal pilot when:
- Stakeholder is at Stage 6 (Budget Discussion) AND has indicated at least nominal budget availability
- Internal Champion Strength ≥ 61 — there is someone inside the organization actively supporting KORA
- Data availability has been confirmed at a basic level
- A budget owner has been part of at least one conversation
- The founder is confident the company's data, though potentially messy, can support a meaningful Foundation Light analysis

Do not propose a pilot to a company where the Internal Champion Strength is below 40 — the champion is not strong enough to sustain the internal approval process. Build the champion first.

### 13.6 When a Pilot Counts as Real Validation

A pilot counts as real (Stage 8 / Active Validator) validation when:
- A formal or semi-formal agreement has been reached — even a written email confirmation of scope and timeline is sufficient
- The company has provided actual data — even in CSV format — for KORA to analyze
- A timeline and at least a minimal success definition have been agreed
- A payment signal has been given, even if nominal or deferred — free pilots that have never involved a price conversation do not qualify

A company that "agreed to a pilot" verbally, has not provided data, and has not discussed commercial terms is at Stage 7 at most (Commitment Signal). Do not count it as Active Validator until data has actually been received.

### 13.7 When to Adjust ICP

Adjust the ICP hypothesis when:
- After 20+ discovery conversations, no sector or size segment shows consistently higher PVS, conversion rates, or WTP signals than the initial hypothesis predicted
- A sector or company profile not in the initial ICP is consistently producing Stage 4+ records with high PVS
- The ICP Fit Distribution (doc 14, Section 4.13) shows that high-ICP-score companies are not converting faster than low-ICP-score companies — meaning the ICP criteria need revision

ICP adjustment is not failure — it is the expected outcome of validation. The first ICP hypothesis is a starting point. What the market teaches over 90 days is worth more than what any analysis produced before first contact.

When adjusting the ICP: update the ICP Fit Score component definitions, re-score all existing records under the new criteria, and note the revision as a version increment in the doc 14 methodology versioning.

### 13.8 When to Stop Chasing Weak Signals

Stop active pursuit and move to Stage 9 when:
- Three or more follow-up contacts have received no response over a period of 60 days
- The stakeholder has explicitly said no, including "not now," "maybe next year," or "we've decided to go another direction"
- PVS has not changed after three separate conversations (value proposition is simply not landing)
- The company has confirmed it does not have the data Foundation Light requires and does not plan to develop it
- ICP Fit Score below 30 — structural misfit that no amount of relationship building will overcome

Moving to Stage 9 is not a defeat — it is a market intelligence event. Populating the Primary Blocker and Objection Tags for Stage 9 records before closing them is mandatory. The Objection Frequency Map is built on lost records as much as won ones.

## 14. Relationship with Doc 14

This playbook is the operational expression of the intelligence framework defined in `docs/14-stakeholder-validation-intelligence-layer.md`. Every metric and decision in this document has a corresponding field, KPI, or signal in doc 14. The relationship is bidirectional: this document defines what to do; doc 14 defines how to measure whether it worked.

### 14.1 ICP Fit Score (doc 14, Section 2.10)

Used in this playbook for: target list prioritization (Section 6), Phase 1 outreach sequencing (Phase 1 of Section 7), the decision rule for when to continue or deprioritize pursuit (Section 13.1, 13.2), and ICP adjustment triggering (Section 13.7).

The ICP Fit Score is the primary prioritization instrument. When bandwidth is constrained, ICP Fit Score determines where time goes. It must be updated after every discovery conversation, not just at record creation.

### 14.2 Perceived Value Score (doc 14, Section 2.6)

Used in this playbook for: signal strength classification (Section 2.1), stage advancement thresholds throughout Section 7, and investor evidence quality assessment (Section 9).

PVS is the primary gauge of value proposition resonance for a given stakeholder. It answers whether KORA is landing — not whether the company is the right type, not whether the relationship is developing, but specifically whether the value proposition is understood and believed. Track PVS changes between interactions — a rising PVS trajectory is more meaningful than a high static PVS.

### 14.3 Trust Score (doc 14, Section 2.7)

Used in this playbook for: signal quality calibration (Section 10.1, 10.2), the decision rule for when to request data sharing (Section 13.3), and investor evidence quality weighting (Section 9.2).

Trust Score acts as a signal reliability multiplier. A high PVS from a low-Trust-Score contact is less valuable than the same PVS from a high-Trust-Score contact. When compiling investor evidence, Trust Score must be considered alongside PVS to assess the credibility of the underlying signals.

### 14.4 Willingness-to-Pay Score (doc 14, Section 5.2)

Used in this playbook for: signal strength hard validation definition (Section 2.1), Phase 4 KPI targets (Phase 4 of Section 7), investor evidence Component 3 (Section 9.2), and the decision rule for pilot proposal timing (Section 13.5).

WTP Score is the commercial validation metric. Without WTP evidence, everything else — PVS, Trust Score, stage progression — is validation of the problem and the solution, but not of the business. A pipeline with high PVS and zero WTP signals is a research project, not a commercial validation.

### 14.5 Internal Champion Strength (doc 14, Section 2.11)

Used in this playbook for: stakeholder map champion identification (Section 5), Phase 3 KPI targets (Phase 3 of Section 7), the false validation risk of no budget owner involvement (Section 10.3), the decision rule for pilot proposal timing (Section 13.5).

Internal Champion Strength is the deal resilience metric. It answers: if the founder stops pushing, will anyone inside the company continue to push for KORA? A deal with no internal champion dies the moment external pressure eases. Building Internal Champion Strength — by providing stakeholders with materials, language, and frameworks to advocate internally — is as important as the external commercial conversation.

### 14.6 Strategic Relationship Value (doc 14, Section 2.12)

Used in this playbook for: advisor channel prioritization (Section 6.1), the decision not to deprioritize high-SRV contacts even when commercial potential is low, and investor evidence Component 8 (repeatable use case) and Component 9 (clear wedge) development.

SRV prevents the playbook from becoming purely revenue-driven in a phase where market positioning, investor narrative, and ecosystem building are as important as near-term commercial outcomes. A company that will never be a paying customer but will generate three investor introductions and provide a case study for KORA's sector credibility is worth more, at this stage, than a paying customer in a non-strategic sector.

### 14.7 Founder Conviction Delta (doc 14, Section 2.9.1)

Used in this playbook for: signal strength interpretation (Section 2.1), false validation risk detection (Section 10), the Phase 2 decision gate (Phase 2 of Section 7), and investor evidence Component 6 (Founder Learning Evidence, Section 9.2).

Founder Conviction Delta is the honest metric. It cannot be faked because it is internal. It measures not what the market said — but what the market taught. A positive cumulative Delta alongside a busy pipeline is evidence that validation is real. A negative or flat cumulative Delta alongside a busy pipeline is evidence that something in the thesis needs examination, regardless of how the pipeline looks.

### 14.8 Validation Confidence Index (doc 14, Section 7.7)

Used in this playbook for: Phase 5 investor evidence pack compilation (Phase 5 of Section 7), the overall assessment of 90-day validation quality.

The Validation Confidence Index is the meta-signal that tells the founder how investor-ready the validation dataset is — not just whether it exists, but whether it is deep enough, diverse enough, and structured enough to support a serious fundraising or partnership conversation. By day 90, the Validation Confidence Index should be at Market Forming (41–60) at minimum. Validation Confirmed (61–80) by day 90 is the target.

## 15. Founder Weekly Review Ritual

Consistent, structured weekly review is the mechanism that prevents the validation intelligence layer from becoming a data archive rather than a decision instrument. The weekly review must happen every week without exception — not when convenient, not when the pipeline looks good. The weeks when the pipeline looks uncertain are the weeks when structured review is most valuable.

### 15.1 Weekly Review Protocol — 60 Minutes Maximum

**Block 1 — Pipeline Changes (15 minutes)**

Review the doc 14 pipeline for changes since last week:
- Which records advanced stage? What was the trigger?
- Which records regressed? What caused the regression?
- Which records are stale (no contact in >14 days)? What is the plan?
- Were any new records created? Are they properly ICP-scored?

Output: updated stage counts, identification of the top three active opportunities requiring action this week.

---

**Block 2 — Signal Review (15 minutes)**

Review the quality of signals received in the past week:
- Top two or three positive signals received — what specifically happened? What does it imply about value proposition, ICP, or messaging?
- Top one or two negative signals — what objection was raised? What did the Founder Conviction Delta log show?
- Which interaction had the highest positive Founder Conviction Delta? What specifically caused it?
- Which interaction had the most negative Founder Conviction Delta? What does it mean?

Output: updated Objection Frequency Map if new objections emerged; revised positioning if a signal pattern is shifting.

---

**Block 3 — ICP Status (10 minutes)**

Review whether the ICP hypothesis is holding:
- Is any sector showing unexpectedly high or low engagement vs. the hypothesis?
- Is any company size tier behaving differently than expected?
- Has any interaction this week produced a strong ICP-fit signal for a company type not in the original hypothesis?

Output: note whether ICP remains confirmed, needs refinement, or triggers a formal review (per decision rule 10.7).

---

**Block 4 — Next Week's Actions (15 minutes)**

Define the three to five most important commercial actions for the coming week:
- Which Stage 6–7 records require the highest attention? What specific next step?
- Which Stage 4–5 records have the highest ICP Fit and need momentum? What is the action?
- Which new outreach targets should be contacted through Priority 1–2 channels?
- Is there any investor stakeholder that requires this week's attention?

Output: a named, dated action list — maximum five items. Not tasks; commitments.

---

**Block 5 — Investor Narrative Update (5 minutes)**

At the end of each week, update one sentence in the investor narrative:
- What is the strongest thing KORA can say this week that it could not say last week?
- Has any new metric reached a threshold that is investor-reportable?

Output: a running investor narrative update log — by the end of 90 days, this log is the foundation of the investor evidence pack.

### 15.2 Weekly Review Cadence and Format

- Day and time: fixed, recurring, non-negotiable. Suggested: Monday morning before any external meetings.
- Duration: maximum 60 minutes. If it takes more than 60 minutes, the intelligence layer is not structured well enough.
- Format: working directly in the doc 14 intelligence layer — not from memory, not from a separate notes document.
- Output: one written summary paragraph logged in a running weekly review file. This becomes part of the validation evidence archive.

### 16.5 Pilot Success Evaluation Criteria

Before any pilot is begun, success must be predefined across four dimensions (see Section 12 for full framework):

| Dimension | Core Criterion | Investor-Reportable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical** | Data received, ingested, classified, outputs generated, methodology traceable | No — prerequisite only |
| **Organizational** | Executive presentation delivered, at least one internal discussion generated, follow-up interest expressed | No — necessary but not sufficient |
| **Commercial** | WTP signal confirmed, next-phase discussion active, budget referenced | Yes — counts as commercial validation |
| **Strategic** | Case study permission, testimonial, referral, investor signaling value | Yes — counts as strategic traction |

**Success level target for the 90-day validation phase:** At least one pilot at Operational Success level. At least one pilot at Strategic Success level. Full Validation Success (paid pilot + case study + referral) is the stretch target.

### 16.6 Strongest Validation Signals

In descending order of evidential weight:

1. A company provides actual data for Foundation Light analysis (Stage 8)
2. A company signs or verbally commits to a paid pilot (Stage 8)
3. A budget owner explicitly discusses pricing and does not disengage (Stage 7)
4. An internal champion introduces KORA to a second internal stakeholder without being asked
5. A stakeholder says "we have this exact problem" without being prompted by the founder
6. A CSRD-obligated ESG lead recognizes KORA as a methodology gap solution
7. An advisor introduces KORA to three or more clients simultaneously

### 16.7 Disqualifying Weak Signals

The following must not be counted as validation, regardless of how promising they feel:

- A stakeholder saying "this is interesting" without a specific follow-up action
- A second meeting agreed to when proposed but not initiated by the stakeholder
- Network contacts enthusiastically endorsing KORA in a social context
- An investor expressing general interest in impact measurement without referencing KORA specifically
- A free pilot accepted without any budget discussion
- A conversation where the problem was discussed but the stakeholder's specific data reality was never explored

### 16.8 Recommended Operating Cadence After 90 Days

After the 90-day validation phase, the operating cadence shifts based on the evidence produced:

**If ≥ 2 Active Validators and ≥ 3 Stage 7 records:** Accelerate to commercial closing. Prioritize converting Stage 7 to Stage 8. Begin investor fundraising process using the evidence pack.

**If 1 Active Validator and ≥ 2 Stage 6 records:** Extend validation by 30 days focused exclusively on converting the Stage 6 records. Do not widen the pipeline — deepen it.

**If 0 Active Validators but a structurally sound Stage 5–6 pipeline:** Diagnose the conversion barrier (pricing, data readiness, champion strength, or urgency). Fix one variable. Test for 30 days.

**If pipeline is broad but shallow (many Stage 3–4, no Stage 5+):** The ICP is too broad or the value proposition is not landing concretely enough. Narrow to the top five highest-ICP-fit records. Rebuild the demo for their specific context. Do not continue scaling outreach.
